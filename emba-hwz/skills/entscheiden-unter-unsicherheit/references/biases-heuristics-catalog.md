# Cognitive Biases & Heuristics — Executive Catalog

This is not the full Wikipedia list of 200 biases. It is the operational subset that meaningfully distorts strategic decisions, with diagnostic questions and concrete counter-moves.

## Heuristics taught in the module

Heuristics are mental shortcuts. They are useful (often correct), but they fail in patterned ways. The three Tversky & Kahneman heuristics are the foundation.

### Availability heuristic
We judge the probability or importance of events by how easily examples come to mind. The recent and the vivid loom larger than the typical.

- **Strategic distortion:** A recent failure of a competitor makes a risk feel imminent; a successful peer makes an opportunity feel obvious.
- **Diagnostic question:** "If we did not have the [recent event] in mind, would we see this the same way?"
- **Counter-move:** Reference-class forecasting — look at the base rate across many cases, not just the one in front of us.

### Representativeness heuristic
We judge how likely something belongs to a category by how much it resembles the prototype, ignoring base rates.

- **Strategic distortion:** "This startup looks like Stripe in 2014, so it will perform like Stripe." Ignores how many startups looked like Stripe and did not perform like Stripe.
- **Diagnostic question:** "What is the base rate of [outcome] for cases that look like this?"
- **Counter-move:** Force a base-rate estimate before discussing case-specific features.

### Anchoring heuristic
The first number on the table pulls subsequent estimates toward it, even when it is arbitrary or irrelevant.

- **Strategic distortion:** Last year's budget anchors this year's. The seller's asking price anchors the buyer's offer. A consultant's estimate anchors the team's planning.
- **Diagnostic question:** "What anchors are in the room? Where did each number come from?"
- **Counter-move:** Independent written estimates before any number is discussed. Multiple anchors deliberately introduced.

## Biases emphasized in the module

### Confirmation bias
We seek, weight, and remember information that confirms our existing beliefs, and discount disconfirming evidence.

- **Strategic distortion:** Once the team has converged on an answer, analysis becomes advocacy. Counter-evidence is rationalized away.
- **Diagnostic question:** "What evidence would change our mind? Have we actively sought it?"
- **Counter-move:** Assign red team to make the strongest case against. Require dissenting view in the decision document.

### Overconfidence
We overestimate the accuracy of our predictions, especially in domains where we feel expert. Three flavors: overestimation (think we are better than we are), overplacement (think we are better than others), overprecision (think our estimates are tighter than they are).

- **Strategic distortion:** Capacity planning, integration timelines, market share forecasts — all systematically optimistic.
- **Diagnostic question:** "On past similar projects, did we hit our forecast? If not, by how much did we miss?"
- **Counter-move:** Reference-class forecasting. Calibration training. Forecasts as ranges, not points. Postmortems with honest accountability.

### Illusion of control
We overestimate our ability to influence outcomes that are largely random or driven by external factors.

- **Strategic distortion:** Believing the success of a launch is mostly due to our team's quality when, in fact, market timing and macro conditions did most of the work. Inverse: blaming the team for outcomes that were structural.
- **Diagnostic question:** "What fraction of the variance in outcomes is realistically under our control?"
- **Counter-move:** Separate skill from luck explicitly in retrospectives. Decision journal that records the decision before outcomes are known.

### Prediction errors (forecast miscalibration)
We are systematically miscalibrated when predicting the future — usually overconfident, sometimes underconfident in specific patterned ways.

- **Strategic distortion:** Long-range plans assume the company will execute through choppy conditions on plan. Reality: most do not.
- **Diagnostic question:** "Have we built our plan around the modal outcome or the full distribution of outcomes?"
- **Counter-move:** Plan for the distribution. Use scenarios (see the `zukunftsforschung` skill for the futures perspective).

## Other biases relevant in strategic decisions

### Sunk cost / escalation of commitment
Persisting because of past investment, even when forward-looking analysis says to stop.

- **Counter-move:** Decisions on continuation should be made as if the project did not yet exist, with the team blind to sunk costs where possible.

### Loss aversion
We weight losses roughly 2x as heavily as equivalent gains. Pushes us toward status-quo conservatism even when expected value favors change.

- **Counter-move:** Reframe options so the status quo is not the default. Force the question: "If we were not already doing X, would we start?"

### Status quo bias
Closely related to loss aversion: a strong preference for things to stay the same.

- **Counter-move:** Pre-commit to revisiting decisions on a schedule. "Default-to-action" framing for reversible decisions.

### Sunk-status bias (specific to executives)
Persisting in a strategy because reversing would damage the executive's status or narrative.

- **Counter-move:** Explicit separation of role and identity. Public commitment to revisiting based on data, not pride.

### Groupthink
Cohesive groups suppress dissent and converge on premature consensus.

- **Counter-move:** Independent assessments before discussion. Structured dissent (devil's advocate, red team). Outside perspectives. Anonymous polling on key questions.

### Halo effect
A strong impression of one feature (e.g., a charismatic founder, a recent win) bleeds into judgments about unrelated features.

- **Counter-move:** Decompose the judgment. Separate scoring on each dimension before forming a holistic view.

### Narrative fallacy
We construct coherent stories about complex events and then mistake the story for understanding.

- **Counter-move:** Ask "what would have to be true for this story to be wrong?" Force alternative narratives.

## How to use this catalog in the workflow

In step 2 of the SKILL.md workflow, do not list all of these. Pick 3–5 that are most likely active in the specific decision in front of the user. For each:

1. Name the bias
2. Describe how it is likely showing up in this decision
3. State the counter-move you are applying in the process

The discipline is surgical relevance, not vocabulary demonstration. Listing biases without changing the process is bias-spotting as performance — it does not improve the decision.
