# Decision Architecture — Beshears & Gino in Practice

The 2015 HBR article by John Beshears and Francesca Gino made a deceptively simple claim: leaders are not just decision-makers, they are decision *architects*. Their primary lever is not making more or better individual decisions; it is shaping the contexts in which decisions get made by others (and by themselves).

This reference operationalizes the architecture toolkit.

## The architect's five principles (Beshears & Gino, adapted)

1. **Understand the system.** Every organizational decision has a context — incentives, defaults, choice architecture, social dynamics. Map them before changing them.
2. **Define the problem.** Most decision failures are framing failures. The architect's first job is to name the actual problem, not the surface symptom.
3. **Build the data and the dissent in.** Set up the inputs (information, alternatives, opposing views) deliberately. The default flow of information is biased toward what is easy to see.
4. **Design the process.** Decisions improve more from better processes than from smarter individuals. The architect designs the steps, not just the outputs.
5. **Run the experiment.** Where possible, test the decision in a small reversible way before committing fully. Even for irreversible decisions, treat the first phase as data collection.

## The architecture toolkit — when and how to use each tool

### Pre-mortem (Klein 2007)
**When:** Before a large irreversible commitment, after the analysis is mostly done but before the decision is locked.

**How:** Convene the decision team. State: "Imagine it is 18 months from now and this decision has failed catastrophically. Each of you, individually, in writing, list the top three reasons it failed." Then consolidate, cluster, and surface the patterns. Often reveals risks that the optimistic analysis suppressed.

**Why it works:** The brain is much better at constructing causal stories than at probabilistic forecasting. Pre-mortem hijacks that capacity for risk identification.

**Watch out for:** Performative pre-mortems where everyone names benign risks. Anonymity helps. Independent writing first, discussion second.

### Red team / Blue team
**When:** High-stakes irreversible decisions where the proposed action has strong internal momentum.

**How:** Split the team. Blue team builds the strongest case for the proposed action. Red team builds the strongest case against, including the most uncomfortable arguments. Both present. Then the decision-maker integrates.

**Why it works:** Counters confirmation bias and groupthink by giving dissent a structural home, not just a tolerated voice.

**Watch out for:** Red team being staffed with junior people whose objections can be safely ignored. Put senior weight on the red team.

### Reference-class forecasting (Kahneman & Tversky → Flyvbjerg)
**When:** Estimating cost, time, or success probability for projects, M&A, or new initiatives.

**How:** Step 1: Define the reference class — a set of comparable past projects, ideally not chosen by the team doing the forecast. Step 2: Establish the base rate distribution (mean and variance of outcomes in the reference class). Step 3: Position this project in the distribution, adjusting for unique features only with explicit justification.

**Why it works:** Inside-view forecasting ("here is our plan") is reliably optimistic. Outside-view forecasting ("here is what happened in comparable cases") corrects.

**Watch out for:** Reference class chosen too narrowly to manipulate the base rate.

### Independent estimates before discussion
**When:** Any meeting where forecasts, probabilities, or estimates will be made.

**How:** Each participant writes their estimate before the conversation. Estimates are revealed simultaneously. *Then* discussion happens.

**Why it works:** Eliminates anchoring on the first opinion expressed, cascade effects where junior people converge to the senior view, and overconfidence reduction from seeing the spread.

**Watch out for:** Treating the average as the answer. The *spread* is often the more important signal.

### Formal devil's advocate
**When:** Decision processes where genuine dissent is not surfacing, often due to seniority dynamics or culture.

**How:** Assign a named, rotating role with explicit permission and expectation to argue the opposing case, even when they personally agree. Compensate them socially for doing it well — never punish.

**Why it works:** Makes dissent low-cost socially. Different from genuine disagreement but useful when the culture suppresses it.

**Watch out for:** Devil's advocate becoming ritualized and easy to dismiss ("Anna always disagrees, that's her job"). Rotate the role.

### Dialectic inquiry
**When:** Strategic choices with two or more viable directions.

**How:** Develop two genuinely different alternatives in equal depth, not one preferred option with token alternatives. Force the team to advocate for each in turn. Only after both are well-developed, choose.

**Why it works:** Most "options analysis" is one option with strawmen. Dialectic inquiry forces real options.

**Watch out for:** Time cost. Reserve for genuinely strategic choices, not routine ones.

### Decision journal
**When:** Continuously, for all material decisions.

**How:** For each significant decision, record before the outcome is known: the decision, the alternatives considered, the key assumptions, the expected outcomes, the confidence level. After the outcome is known, return to the entry and assess — separating decision quality from outcome quality.

**Why it works:** Without a journal, hindsight rewrites history. We remember our good decisions as wise and our bad ones as obvious in retrospect. The journal preserves the actual epistemic state.

**Watch out for:** Becoming a chore. Keep entries short — five sentences is fine.

### Calibration training
**When:** Teams that make repeated forecasts (R&D, sales, strategy).

**How:** Train the team to give probability estimates with calibration scoring. Over many forecasts, track whether their 80% confidence intervals contain the truth ~80% of the time. Most untrained executives are dramatically overconfident; calibration improves measurably with practice.

**Why it works:** Makes overconfidence visible and trainable.

**Watch out for:** Treating calibration as a personal accusation. Frame it as a team capability.

## Choosing the right tool

Match the architecture investment to the decision:

| Decision class | Tools to use |
|----------------|--------------|
| Routine operational | Light architecture: clear defaults, single-page rationale |
| Tactical but reversible | Decision journal, independent estimates |
| Strategic but reversible | Pre-mortem, dialectic inquiry, decision journal |
| Strategic irreversible | Pre-mortem, red team, reference-class forecasting, formal dissent, decision journal |
| Crisis-time | Smaller team, faster cycle, independent estimates, named decision-maker, post-mortem afterward |

## How to use this in the workflow

In step 3 of the SKILL.md workflow, pick 2–4 tools appropriate to the decision class. Apply them. State explicitly in the decision artifact which architecture tools were used and what they surfaced — this builds the user's track record of taking decision quality seriously, which compounds.
