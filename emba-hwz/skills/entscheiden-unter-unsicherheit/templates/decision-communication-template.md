# Decision Communication Template

How to communicate a decision to a team or organization so that it is understood, accepted, and learnable from later. Decisions die in the communication step more often than in the deliberation step.

## Structure

### 1. The decision — first sentence
> _"We are doing X, starting [date], owned by [name]."_

Do not bury the lede. Do not start with context. Lead with the call.

### 2. The why — second paragraph
> _The single most important reason for the choice. Not a list. The one thing._

If you cannot state the why in one paragraph, you do not yet have a decision — you have a compromise.

### 3. What we considered and chose not to do
> _The 1–2 alternatives that were seriously considered, and why we did not pick them._

This step does more work than any other. It tells the team that the decision was reasoned, not arbitrary. It also forecloses second-guessing of options that were already considered.

### 4. What we are uncertain about
> _The 1–2 places where the analysis is thinnest. Naming this builds credibility — pretending certainty corrodes it._

### 5. What could change the decision
> _The observable conditions that would force re-evaluation. Stating these protects credibility when the world shifts and the decision changes._

### 6. What happens next
> _Concrete actions, owners, dates. The first three things that visibly happen to demonstrate the decision is real._

### 7. The standing offer
> _"If you see [signal] or have [concern], here is how to raise it: [channel, name]."_

This keeps the decision live and learnable rather than crystallizing into dogma.

## Tone principles

- **Be short.** A one-page memo or a 4-minute town hall slot. Past that, the message dilutes.
- **Use first person plural sparingly.** "We" can hide who decided. Sometimes "I have decided" is the more honest form.
- **Name losses.** If the decision involves giving something up (or up on), say so. Pretending nothing was lost insults the team.
- **Avoid heroic language.** Decisions made in genuine uncertainty are not heroic. They are responsible. Heroic framing creates pressure to be right rather than to be honest later.
- **Avoid managementese.** "Pivot," "double down," "going forward" — every empty phrase taxes credibility.

## Common failure modes

- **Over-explaining.** A 10-slide deck explaining why is usually defensive. If the decision is right, two minutes is enough.
- **Buried decision.** Leading with context loses the room before the decision lands.
- **False certainty.** Claiming we know things we don't burns goodwill when reality intrudes.
- **Closed loop.** Communicating the decision once and never returning. Decisions need a learning loop visible to the team that made them.

## Example skeleton (for a difficult organizational decision)

> "Starting Q3, we are closing the [Region] sales office and consolidating to [HQ]. This affects 18 colleagues; we will support them with [package, timing, named owner].
>
> We are making this call because [single most important reason — typically a business reality, not a strategic abstraction]. The numbers are in the appendix; the short version is that the unit economics no longer work.
>
> We seriously considered keeping the office open with a smaller footprint, and we considered relocating it rather than closing it. We chose not to because [specific reason for rejecting each].
>
> What we are uncertain about: [the one or two assumptions where we may be wrong]. If [observable signal] happens by [date], we will revisit.
>
> What happens now: [first three concrete actions with owners and dates]. If you have concerns or see something we should know, [name] is the named channel; please reach out directly."

That is enough. Trust the team to read carefully. Resist the urge to over-explain.
