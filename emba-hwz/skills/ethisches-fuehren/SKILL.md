# Ethisches und Unethisches Führen — Understanding the Mechanisms

This skill operationalizes EMBA Block 6 themenübergreifend (Prof. Guido Palazzo, HWZ). It is the integrating skill of the program because its question runs through every module: how do organizations of generally good people produce systematic ethical failure?

Palazzo's central insight, supported by decades of research and case study (his own work on ethical blindness; the patterns across Volkswagen, Boeing, Wirecard, Credit Suisse, Theranos, Uber, FTX): the perpetrators of corporate scandals are almost never bad people. They are people with values and good intentions, operating inside systems that produce predictable distortions of perception, judgment, and action.

The work is to understand the mechanisms — so you can see them coming, in your own decisions and in your organization. And to build the personal and organizational practices that resist them.

## When to lean on this skill

Use it when the user is: facing an ethical dilemma; building or running an integrity / compliance / ethics program; analyzing why a scandal happened (theirs or others'); designing speak-up infrastructure; coaching a team member on a hard ethical situation; conducting a post-mortem; or simply trying to keep their own moral compass straight in a demanding environment.

Do not use it as a substitute for: specific legal advice on a violation; whistleblower support that needs professional channels; or therapy for someone in genuine moral injury. Refer where appropriate.

## Mental model: ethical blindness as the default risk

Three integrating ideas:

1. **Ethical failure is almost never about bad apples.** The "rogue actor" narrative comforts the rest of us but rarely explains the pattern. The mechanisms are systemic and predictable.

2. **Ethical blindness happens to people who would, in another context, see clearly.** Under specific conditions — time pressure, in-group dynamics, framing as technical-not-ethical, gradual escalation, ambiguous responsibility — humans systematically fail to perceive the ethical dimension of what they are doing. They are not making bad choices; they are not seeing the choice.

3. **Speak-up and Listen-up are the operating practices that resist the pattern.** Without functioning channels for bad news to travel upward and for leaders to hear it well, the early warning signs that precede every scandal go undetected. The infrastructure has to be designed and maintained.

## Workflow

### 1. Understand the patterns

Read `references/scandal-patterns.md`. Across the major scandals, the same patterns recur:

- **Time pressure** producing shortcut-taking
- **In-group loyalty** suppressing dissent
- **Goal escalation** (we set numbers that can only be met by cutting corners)
- **Diffusion of responsibility** (everyone followed procedure / their boss / the legal team)
- **Framing the ethical as technical** (it was an accounting choice; an engineering trade-off; a business decision)
- **Gradual desensitization** (each small step was within the ordinary; the cumulative was catastrophic)
- **Authority deference** (powerful figures' judgment trusted over evidence)
- **Reward systems** that punished honesty and rewarded creative compliance

Naming the patterns is itself protective. Once seen, they are harder to repeat unconsciously.

### 2. Diagnose ethical blindness in your context

Read `references/ethical-blindness-mechanisms.md`. The Palazzo / Krings / Hoffrage (2012) framework. The conditions that produce ethical blindness in otherwise normal people:

- **Self-deception** — convenient reframing of what is actually happening
- **Routine and gradualism** — small steps that don't feel ethical
- **Strong frames** — financial / technical / competitive framing that excludes the ethical
- **In-group dynamics** — loyalty to colleagues over independent judgment
- **Anonymous others** — when victims are abstract, ethical attention diminishes

For each, the user can audit their own organization: where are these conditions present? They are present everywhere in some degree; the question is what is being done about them.

### 3. Apply the diagnostic to a specific decision or situation

Use `templates/ethical-blindness-check.md`. For a real decision the user is facing or observing, run the diagnostic:

- What time pressure is operating?
- What in-group is being protected?
- What goal is being chased, and at what cost?
- Whose voice is being suppressed?
- What is being framed as technical that is actually ethical?
- How has the team's normal-zone shifted in recent months/years?
- What would a trusted outsider, fully briefed, say about this?

The diagnostic produces uncomfortable insights. The discomfort is the work.

### 4. Build speak-up practice

Read `references/speak-up.md`. As an employee or junior leader, the question: how do I raise concerns effectively? The Palazzo / module materials emphasize that speaking up is itself a skill:

- Choose the moment, the channel, the framing deliberately
- Frame as inquiry rather than accusation when possible
- Start with people who have power to act, not those who don't
- Document; have a paper trail
- Build alliances before escalating publicly
- Understand the personal risk and decide deliberately

Speak-up is not just "say what you think." It is a craft for getting concerns heard and addressed.

### 5. Build listen-up practice

Read `references/listen-up.md`. As a senior leader, the question: how do I make sure I hear what I need to hear? The infrastructure of listen-up:

- Multiple channels for concerns to reach you, including bypassing direct reports
- Visible response to past concerns, even small ones
- Personal modeling — admitting mistakes, asking questions, naming dissent
- Trained managers who can themselves listen-up at their layer
- Periodic temperature-taking that doesn't rely on volunteered information
- Genuine no-retaliation discipline, tested under stress

Listen-up is more important than speak-up: speak-up requires brave employees; listen-up requires courageous leaders willing to act on uncomfortable information.

### 6. Run the dilemma analysis when stuck

Read `references/dilemma-analysis.md`. For genuine ethical dilemmas (not just hard choices), a structured analysis:

- What are the values in tension?
- Who is affected, and how?
- What principle does each option honor; what does each violate?
- What does the person of integrity I aspire to be do here?
- What would I be comfortable defending publicly?
- What does the regret test reveal (which option will I regret more, and why)?

The dilemma analysis does not produce a clean answer for all dilemmas. It produces an honest engagement that itself has integrity.

### 7. Build the organizational integrity infrastructure

Use `templates/integrity-program-design.md`. The components of a working integrity program:

- **Code that lives** — not just on the wall, but tested in actual decisions
- **Speak-up channels** that work in practice, not just policy
- **Investigation discipline** — fast, fair, documented
- **Sanctions that bite** — visible accountability, including for the powerful
- **Education that addresses real situations** — not generic compliance training
- **Leadership behavior** — the senior team's lived integrity is the program

Compliance is necessary but insufficient. The integrity program is broader and harder.

## Output style

Honest, non-moralistic, technically careful. Avoid both the cynical mode ("everyone is corrupt") and the moralistic mode ("just be good people"). The truth is harder: good people fail predictably under specific conditions; understanding the conditions and building the practices that resist them is the work.

Use specific examples from the major scandals where helpful. The pattern recognition is itself the contribution.

## Pitfalls to avoid

- **Bad-apple framing.** "Just remove the bad people and the system is fine." Misses the systemic causes; produces the next scandal in the same place.
- **Code-on-the-wall theater.** A beautiful code of conduct that no one consults in actual decisions.
- **Compliance-as-ethics.** Compliance is necessary; it is not sufficient. Many legal activities are unethical; many ethical activities are not directly required by law.
- **Whistleblower handling as PR problem.** Treating speak-up as reputational risk to manage rather than information to learn from. Predictable failure.
- **Ethics training as box-tick.** Annual generic training that no one applies. Use scenario-based, real-situation training.
- **Senior leadership exempt.** Standards for everyone except the top. Visible to all employees; corrodes trust.
- **Moralizing.** Treating ethics as obvious when the actual situation is complex. Drives genuine ethical struggle underground.
