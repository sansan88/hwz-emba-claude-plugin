# Ethical Dilemma Analysis

Sometimes a situation is not "right vs. wrong" — it is "right vs. right," where two values both have legitimate claims and the choice will cost something. These are the dilemmas worth analyzing carefully. The work is not to find the painless answer (often there isn't one) but to engage the dilemma with integrity.

This reference is for those moments.

## Distinguishing dilemma from temptation

Before deep analysis, check whether the situation is actually a dilemma:

- **Dilemma:** Two legitimate values in tension. Either choice has real cost. The right answer is genuinely hard to identify.
- **Temptation:** You know the right answer. The question is whether you will act on it. The hard thing is the willpower, not the reasoning.

Most "dilemmas" people bring to ethics conversations are actually temptations dressed up. The honest first move: is this a dilemma, or am I looking for permission?

If temptation, the work is willpower, accountability, and structural support to act on what you already know.

If genuine dilemma, the structured analysis below.

## The structured analysis

### 1. State the situation clearly

Without framing it for a particular answer. Describe what is happening, who is involved, what the decision is, what the deadline is.

> _"On Friday I have to decide whether to [X] or [Y]. The context is..."_

### 2. Identify the values in tension

What values does each option honor? What values does each option violate?

| Option A | Honors | Violates |
|----------|--------|----------|
| | | |
| Option B | | |

If you struggle to identify values violated by each option, it may not be a dilemma — see distinguishing above.

### 3. Identify who is affected

Specifically, by name where possible. The abstract list of "stakeholders" produces less ethical attention than specific people.

- Person A: how affected
- Person B: how affected
- Group C: how affected

Including affected parties not in the room — future employees, distant customers, communities, future selves.

### 4. Apply multiple ethical lenses

Different ethical traditions weigh different things. Run each as a check:

- **Consequentialist** — which option produces the better outcomes, overall, for those affected?
- **Deontological** — which option honors the duties and rights involved?
- **Virtue** — which option does a person of good character take?
- **Care** — which option honors the relationships and responsibilities involved?
- **Justice** — which option produces fairer distribution of benefit and burden?

Different traditions sometimes converge, sometimes diverge. Where they converge, your confidence in the answer can be higher. Where they diverge, the dilemma is real.

### 5. The trusted-others tests

Several useful imagined-other tests:

- **The mentor test** — what would my most respected mentor advise?
- **The successor test** — what would I want my successor to do if she inherited this situation?
- **The press test** — what would I be comfortable defending publicly?
- **The future-self test** — which version of this will I be glad to have done in 5 years?

These tests work by introducing perspectives that escape current pressures.

### 6. The regret test

Imagine you have chosen Option A. Five years from now, walking past your old office, you reflect. What regret might you carry?

Now imagine you have chosen Option B. Same exercise.

Which regret is harder to live with? Which option produces the more easily-borne regret?

Not always definitive, but often clarifying.

### 7. The integrity test

Which option is consistent with the kind of person you are trying to be?

This is not about being right or popular. It is about coherence between your values and your action. A choice that violates your core identity may be defensible, but it will cost you. Sometimes the cost is worth it; sometimes it isn't. Be clear-eyed.

### 8. The "what would justify the worse-seeming option" test

Often one option looks worse on the surface. Push: what would have to be true to make it the better choice? Is any of that actually true? This pressure-tests the apparent answer.

### 9. The acknowledgment of cost

A genuine dilemma has costs in both directions. Name what you are giving up in the option you choose. Don't pretend the choice is costless.

This honest acknowledgment is itself integrity — it distinguishes "I made the harder right choice and am giving up X" from "I made the convenient choice and am pretending it cost nothing."

### 10. The action

Decide. Act. Document your reasoning briefly for yourself.

## What dilemma analysis does not produce

- It does not produce certainty. Genuine dilemmas don't have clean answers.
- It does not eliminate cost. The option you don't choose still had value.
- It does not protect you from criticism. Even the well-reasoned choice can be second-guessed.

What it does produce: a decision you can stand behind, made with awareness of what was at stake, with the honesty that distinguishes integrity from convenience.

## Common dilemma failure patterns

### Treating a dilemma as a temptation
Pretending the answer is obvious when it isn't. Either denies the genuine difficulty or seeks permission for a foregone choice.

### Treating a temptation as a dilemma
Generating arguments for the temptation as if it were a hard ethical question. Self-deception.

### Analysis paralysis
Using analysis to defer decision indefinitely. The deferral is itself a choice.

### Lone analysis
Important dilemmas often benefit from trusted counsel. A mentor, an ethics committee, a trusted peer. Not to outsource the decision — to widen the analysis.

### Lawyering the dilemma
Treating the ethics question as a compliance question. Compliance has different scope; many legal choices are unethical and many unethical things aren't legally bright lines.

### Performing the dilemma
Going through the motions of analysis to produce the answer you already wanted. Detected by you (eventually) and others.

## The role of the leader in others' dilemmas

When you are the senior person to whom someone brings a dilemma:

- Don't solve it for them
- Listen more than you advise
- Help them surface the values and stakes
- Be honest about the costs of each option
- Support them in the analysis without pushing toward a particular answer
- Respect that the decision is theirs

The exception: when the dilemma involves their staying or going (whistleblowing, conscientious objection), the support may involve helping them prepare for the consequences of the choice they make.

## How to use this in the workflow

In step 6 of the SKILL.md workflow, walk a specific dilemma through this structure. Resist the temptation to short-circuit to an answer. The structure produces decisions that can be lived with; the rush produces decisions that haunt.
