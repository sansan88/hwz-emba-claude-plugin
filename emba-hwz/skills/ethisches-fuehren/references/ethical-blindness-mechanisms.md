# Ethical Blindness — The Mechanisms

The central concept from Palazzo, Krings, & Hoffrage (2012). Ethical blindness is the temporary inability to perceive the ethical dimensions of a situation — happening to people who would normally see clearly. It is not moral failure in the simple sense; it is a perceptual failure that produces moral failure as a consequence.

Understanding the mechanisms is the first line of defense, because once you can name them, you can notice them operating.

## The five mechanisms

### 1. Self-deception

The brain is excellent at constructing accounts of behavior that protect self-concept. Faced with conflicting values, it does not consciously choose to violate one; it constructs an account in which the violation is invisible or justified.

Examples:
- "I'm not lying; I'm just framing it strategically."
- "I'm not exploiting; I'm offering an opportunity."
- "I'm not silencing; I'm focusing the conversation."

The self-deceiver believes their account. The cognitive dissonance is resolved without conscious choice.

**Mitigation:** explicit naming, periodically. "What would a trusted outsider call what I am actually doing here?" The discipline of naming honestly cuts through self-deception.

### 2. Routine and gradualism

Each small step is normal. Each next step is only slightly larger than the previous. There is no moment of crossing a line because the line is approached incrementally over months or years.

The Volkswagen defeat device evolved over a decade. Boeing's MCAS modifications happened iteratively. Wirecard's books drifted further from reality each quarter.

**Mitigation:** periodic ground-truthing against original intent. "If we showed our practices three years ago to ourselves today, what would we think?" The cumulative is visible even when each step is invisible.

### 3. Strong frames

A frame is the implicit context through which we interpret a situation. Frames are normally helpful — they let us act in complex environments. They become dangerous when they exclude relevant dimensions.

Financial frame: this is an accounting question.
Technical frame: this is an engineering trade-off.
Competitive frame: this is what the market requires.
Legal frame: this is what the lawyers approved.

Each frame may be partially correct. Each can exclude the ethical dimension if it is the dominant frame.

**Mitigation:** explicit multi-frame analysis. "What are the financial, technical, ethical, human, long-term, and stakeholder dimensions of this?" Force the ethical lens into the conversation.

### 4. In-group dynamics

Humans evolved in tight groups. The brain still privileges in-group cohesion. Disagreeing with the in-group is socially costly; conforming is socially rewarded.

In tight executive teams, the in-group becomes the executive team. Dissent against the team is dissent against belonging. The brain solves this by adjusting perception — the team becomes right, the dissenter becomes wrong.

**Mitigation:** structural dissent (red teams, designated devil's advocates); external voices; periodic membership refresh; explicit valuing of dissent.

### 5. Anonymous others

Empathy is activated by specific, identified persons. Statistical, aggregate, or abstract victims do not activate the same response. "200 customers harmed" produces less ethical attention than "Anna, age 8, harmed."

Many corporate ethical failures involve harms to abstract groups — markets, regulators, communities, future customers, employees-in-aggregate. The abstraction itself reduces ethical attention.

**Mitigation:** specific imagination of affected parties. "Picture the specific person who is harmed by this. Name them. Imagine the conversation with them." Re-introduces the empathy circuit.

## The compound effect

These mechanisms compound. A team operating under pressure (compresses reflection), with strong financial framing (excludes ethical dimension), high in-group cohesion (suppresses dissent), gradually escalating practices (each step routine), affecting anonymous victims (no empathy activation) — produces ethical blindness reliably. The participants are not unusually bad. The conditions are unusually conducive.

This is what makes the mechanisms important. They explain why ordinary people, in ordinary roles, produce extraordinary moral failure.

## How they show up in everyday business

Not just in scandals. Every-day examples:

- The performance review framed as "fair process" that ignores how it actually affects the individual
- The cost-saving that "everyone's doing" but harms a specific group of stakeholders
- The marketing claim that is "technically true" but misleading
- The negotiation tactic that exploits a customer's weak position
- The headcount decision framed as "business necessity" without ethical reflection

These are not scandals. They are the soil in which scandals grow. Practice on the small ones builds the muscle for the big ones.

## The leadership implication

Senior leaders are particularly vulnerable to ethical blindness:

- More pressure, less time for reflection
- More invested in current frames
- Stronger in-group dynamics at the senior level
- Greater distance from affected parties

The implication: senior leaders need *more* structural support for ethical perception, not less. Self-reliance is the wrong model; structural humility is the right one.

## The discipline

Building the perceptual capacity to notice ethical dimensions is a discipline. Specific practices:

- **Periodic ethical audit** of own recent decisions — what was at stake that I didn't fully engage?
- **External advisors** — people who will say what insiders won't
- **The "trusted outsider" question** — what would they see?
- **Specific affected-person imagination** — who is hurt by this, named, imagined?
- **The "publish the email" test** — if my reasoning here were published, what would happen?
- **Time delay** before consequential decisions — overnight reflection on what was framed as urgent

These practices look like overhead. They are the cheapest form of ethical risk management.

## How to use this in the workflow

In step 2 of the SKILL.md workflow, run the mechanism diagnostic for the user's context or for a specific decision they are facing. Identify which mechanisms are most active. Apply the corresponding mitigations.

Ethical blindness is not a character flaw. It is a feature of cognition under specific conditions. Understanding it makes it predictable, and predictable patterns can be designed against.
