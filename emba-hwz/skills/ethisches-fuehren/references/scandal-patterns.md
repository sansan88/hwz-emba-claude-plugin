# The Patterns Across the Big Scandals

Volkswagen, Boeing 737 MAX, Credit Suisse, Wirecard, Theranos, Uber, FTX, and others. The lay narrative is "bad people did bad things." The systematic study reveals patterns that repeat across companies, industries, and decades. Recognizing the patterns is the first line of defense.

## Pattern 1 — Aggressive goal-setting that can only be met dishonestly

Almost every major scandal has unrealistic targets in its backstory. Volkswagen needed to win on diesel emissions; Boeing needed to compete with Airbus on time and budget; Wells Fargo needed sales numbers per branch.

When the only paths to the target are honest-and-impossible or dishonest-and-doable, the dishonest paths predictably get walked. The senior leaders who set the targets often did not explicitly authorize the dishonesty; they made it inevitable by setting the targets and tolerating the results.

**Watch for:** Goals that internal experts say privately are unmeetable; "stretch goals" that become non-negotiable; cascading targets without cascading enabling capability.

## Pattern 2 — Pressure compresses the ethical reflection time

Time pressure narrows attention. Under pressure, the brain selects from familiar patterns rather than reasoning broadly. Ethical considerations require slower thinking; they get crowded out.

Volkswagen engineers had a deadline. Boeing had a deadline. Theranos had funding cycles. The pressure was real; the response was to cut the ethical corners that took too long to honor.

**Watch for:** Decisions that "have to be made now;" senior leaders impatient with thorough analysis; consistent operating tempo that excludes time for deliberation.

## Pattern 3 — In-group loyalty over independent judgment

Cohesive teams produce belonging — and conformity. In high-cohesion teams, dissent is socially costly. People who see the problem either suppress it, leave, or get pushed out.

Wirecard's executive team had become a closed loyalty group. Theranos had the same. Credit Suisse's investment bankers protected each other from compliance scrutiny.

**Watch for:** Long-tenured executive teams with little outside input; departure of independent voices; rituals of loyalty visible above competence.

## Pattern 4 — Gradual escalation

The bright line was never crossed in a single step. It was crossed in many small steps, each one only slightly past the previous, each one explainable in context.

Boeing 737 MAX MCAS modifications happened iteratively. Volkswagen's defeat device got more elaborate over years. FTX's commingling started small.

**Watch for:** "We've always done it this way;" practices that have drifted from where they started; lack of periodic ground-truthing against the original intent.

## Pattern 5 — Diffusion of responsibility

When responsibility is spread across many people, no one feels it. Each can claim they were following procedure, doing their part, trusting their superior, trusting the system.

The Volkswagen engineers said they followed instructions. The Theranos lab staff said they trusted Elizabeth Holmes. The Boeing safety engineers said they raised concerns and were overruled.

**Watch for:** Complex decision processes where no single person owns the outcome; "everyone touched it but no one owned it" patterns; layered approvals that produce ambiguity rather than accountability.

## Pattern 6 — Reframing the ethical as technical / business / political

The most consequential pattern. The actual situation involves ethical dimensions; the framing systematically excludes them.

- Volkswagen: "It was an engineering optimization for emissions testing."
- Boeing: "It was an aerodynamic correction."
- Wirecard: "It was an aggressive accounting approach."
- Theranos: "It was a development-stage product, not a clinical one."

Each framing is partially true. None engages the ethical dimension. The framing is what produced the blindness.

**Watch for:** Decisions categorized purely in financial / technical / legal / political language; ethical considerations explicitly named only by junior people or outsiders; the recurring sense that "we discussed it" without discussing the actual stakes.

## Pattern 7 — Authority deference

When powerful figures express views, dissent becomes harder. The cognitive effort to disagree is real; the social cost is real; the career risk is real.

Theranos had Holmes. Wirecard had Marsalek and Braun. FTX had Bankman-Fried. Each was treated by surrounding people as an authority whose judgment trumped contradicting evidence.

**Watch for:** Charismatic leaders whose views are not effectively challenged; junior employees deferring even when they have relevant expertise; boards that operate as fan clubs.

## Pattern 8 — Reward systems that punish honesty

The cultures that produced scandals often punished people who raised concerns and rewarded creative compliance. Whistleblowers were marginalized; the people who got results "no matter how" were promoted.

Wells Fargo, Wirecard, Theranos, Credit Suisse, Boeing — all had documented patterns of penalizing dissent and rewarding aggressive compliance.

**Watch for:** Recent departures of people who raised concerns; compensation outcomes that reward results over how they were achieved; visible career penalties for unpopular truth-telling.

## Pattern 9 — Anonymous victims

When the people harmed are abstract, distant, or invisible, ethical attention diminishes. "The market" cannot be hurt; "regulators" cannot be hurt; "future customers" cannot be hurt.

Volkswagen's emissions affected children with asthma — but invisibly. Boeing's plane failures killed people, but in distant places, until the second crash and the cumulative pattern.

**Watch for:** Decisions where the affected parties are not specifically named or imagined; decisions where the harm is statistical rather than visible.

## Pattern 10 — Speak-up failure precedes the scandal

In every major scandal, multiple people knew at least some of what was happening. The information existed in the organization. The pattern is the failure of that information to reach those who could act on it.

The infrastructure of speak-up (channels, protections, response) was missing or broken. The infrastructure of listen-up (curiosity, willingness to hear, action on what was heard) was missing or broken.

**Watch for:** Speak-up data that shows no usage (might be no problems; more often indicates broken trust); retaliation cases handled quietly; "we have a hotline" used as a substitute for a working system.

## How to use this in the workflow

In step 1 of the SKILL.md workflow, walk through these patterns with the user. For each, ask: is this pattern present in your organization, even in mild form? Most companies have several. The honest acknowledgment is the start of the work.

These patterns are not abstract. They are how the next scandal will look. The companies that recognize them in time are the ones that avoid becoming case studies.
