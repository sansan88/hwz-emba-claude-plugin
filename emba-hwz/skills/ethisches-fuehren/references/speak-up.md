# Speak-Up — The Craft of Raising Concerns Effectively

Speaking up is a skill. Done well, it changes outcomes and protects the speaker. Done badly, it produces casualties without changes. The difference is not courage — courage is necessary but not sufficient. The difference is craft.

Palazzo's module treats speak-up as a learnable practice. This reference operationalizes it.

## What speak-up is

Speak-up is the act of raising a concern about something you observe that you believe is wrong, problematic, or risky — to someone with the capacity to address it. It is not whistle-blowing in the legal sense (though it can escalate to that). It is the everyday practice of professional integrity.

Speak-up includes:

- Naming a problem in a meeting where everyone is acting as if there isn't one
- Telling a colleague you disagree with a decision they are about to make
- Raising a concern with your manager
- Pushing back on a senior leader's directive
- Flagging an issue to compliance, legal, or ethics
- Going external (regulator, media) when internal channels fail

Each form has different stakes and different craft.

## The decision to speak

Before speaking, an honest assessment:

### Is this worth speaking up about?
Not every disagreement requires speak-up. The threshold:
- Is there real harm, risk, or wrongdoing — not just a different preference?
- Is the harm large enough to justify the cost of speaking?
- Am I in a position to know enough to be confident in the concern?

If yes to all three, the question becomes how, not whether.

### What is my goal?
Different goals require different approaches:
- **Inform** — make sure decision-makers have the information
- **Influence** — shape the decision
- **Protect** — create a record so I'm not later complicit
- **Stop** — prevent the action entirely

Be honest about which. The approach differs.

### Am I willing to bear the cost?
Speak-up has costs — social, career, sometimes financial. Be clear about what you are willing to accept. There is no shame in deciding the cost is too high in a specific situation; pretending you'll speak up when you won't is worse.

## The craft

### Choose the moment
Not all moments are equal. Considerations:

- **Before a decision is locked in** — much easier to influence than to reverse
- **Privately first, often** — public challenge raises stakes for the listener
- **When you have data** — concerns landed with evidence get further than concerns landed with intuition
- **Not in extremis** — speaking up when your career is already at risk reads as desperate

### Choose the channel
Channels in escalating order:

1. **Direct conversation** with the person doing the thing
2. **Direct conversation** with their manager or peer
3. **Senior leader** who has authority to act
4. **HR / compliance / ethics** if the issue is systemic or involves the person above
5. **Board or board committee** for senior-level issues
6. **Regulatory or external** when internal channels fail or are themselves implicated

Each escalation should usually only happen if the previous step has been tried and failed, unless the urgency or severity demands skipping.

### Choose the framing
Three useful framings, often combined:

- **Curiosity / inquiry framing** — "I want to understand X better; here's what I'm seeing that doesn't fit." Lower resistance than accusation.
- **Risk framing** — "I'm worried about [specific risk]." Speaks the language of risk management.
- **Values framing** — "I want to make sure we're operating consistent with our stated values around X." Speaks the language of integrity.

Avoid pure accusation framing — it activates defensiveness and reduces the chance of a productive conversation.

### Document
Whatever the outcome, document:

- What you observed
- When and to whom you raised it
- What response you received
- What action (or non-action) followed

The documentation protects you (you can show you raised it) and creates a record that may matter later. Keep copies outside company systems where appropriate.

### Build alliances
Speak-up is more powerful when not solo. Before escalating:

- Identify others who share the concern
- Decide whether to act together or sequentially
- Make sure key allies know you are speaking up; they may not feel they can lead but can support

Solo speak-up is sometimes necessary. When allies are available, leverage them.

### Manage the personal risk

Concrete actions:

- Document continuously (see above)
- Keep performance excellent (makes retaliation harder to justify)
- Build external network in case career impact materializes
- Know the relevant laws (whistleblower protections vary by jurisdiction)
- Seek counsel before major escalation if stakes are high
- Be ready to leave if the system retaliates and won't address it

## What good speak-up looks like

A few characteristics of effective speak-up:

- **Specific** — not "things feel off here" but "the practice of X is producing Y, which conflicts with our stated values / regulatory requirements / customer interests"
- **Evidenced** — observations, not just feelings
- **Constructive** — names what could be done, not just what is wrong
- **Sustained** — comes back if the first raising doesn't land
- **Calibrated** — appropriate seriousness without melodrama

## When speak-up fails

Sometimes you speak up, properly, and nothing changes. The system you are speaking to is itself part of the problem. Options:

- **Persist** through additional channels
- **Escalate** to external bodies (regulator, professional association, sometimes media)
- **Leave** rather than be complicit
- **Stay and document** — sometimes the next moment for action will come

The honest acknowledgment: speak-up does not always work. The discipline is to do it well, to know what you are willing to bear, and to live with the outcome.

## The leader's responsibility

If you are the leader to whom others speak up, your behavior is the speak-up infrastructure. See `listen-up.md` for the corresponding craft.

The single most important thing a leader can do for speak-up: visibly reward (or at minimum not punish) people who have spoken up about concerns. The history of how the last speak-up was handled determines the next one.

## How to use this in the workflow

In step 4 of the SKILL.md workflow, work with the user on a specific speak-up they are considering — or have already done badly and want to revisit. Design the moment, channel, framing, documentation, alliances, and personal risk management. The work is craft; with practice, it improves.

Most consequential corporate failures had multiple people who saw the problem. The companies that avoid the failures are the ones where speak-up worked. The infrastructure is built by people who do the work well.
