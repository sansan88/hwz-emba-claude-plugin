# Circular Business Models — Patterns and Decisions

The deepest sustainability impact comes from changing the business model — what the company sells, how it makes it, how it captures value. This reference operationalizes the major circular business model patterns and when each fits.

## Why circularity matters strategically

The linear economy (take → make → use → dispose) drives the resource and emissions throughput that the IPAT equation captures. A circular economy aims to design out waste and keep materials in use at their highest value for as long as possible.

For a company, the strategic implications go beyond "sustainability":

- **Resource cost protection** — circular models reduce exposure to virgin material price volatility
- **Regulation tailwinds** — EU Circular Economy Action Plan, right-to-repair, Ecodesign for Sustainable Products Regulation push the field
- **Customer pull** — B2B procurement increasingly requires recycled content, repairability
- **New revenue pools** — service revenues, remanufactured product margins, secondary materials trading

## The pattern catalog

### Pattern 1 — Product-as-a-Service (PaaS)
Sell the outcome or usage rather than the product. Customer pays per use, per period, per outcome.

Examples:
- Lighting-as-a-service (Philips Signify)
- Mobility-as-a-service (Lyft, Lime)
- Equipment leasing with maintenance and end-of-life return

**Fits when:** the product is durable, has high upfront cost, has maintenance complexity, and the customer values outcome over ownership.

**Why it is circular:** the provider retains ownership and so has direct incentive to design for durability, repairability, and end-of-life recovery.

**Strategic implications:** balance sheet changes, revenue recognition shifts, working capital, service capability buildup, customer expectations.

### Pattern 2 — Refurbishment / Remanufacturing
Take back used products, restore to as-new condition, resell.

Examples:
- Caterpillar remanufactured engines
- Refurbished consumer electronics (Apple, Samsung)
- Tire retreading

**Fits when:** the core product has long usable life, the wear is in identifiable replaceable components, and a market exists for refurbished units.

**Why it is circular:** extends product life, retains material and embedded labor value.

**Strategic implications:** reverse logistics infrastructure, refurbishment operations, possible cannibalization concerns (usually overblown).

### Pattern 3 — Material recovery
Recover materials at end of life for use in new production.

Examples:
- Aluminum recycling (>90% recoverable; saves ~95% of energy vs. primary)
- Cement clinker substitution with recycled aggregates
- Plastic recycling (much harder than aluminum due to contamination and degradation)

**Fits when:** the material is recoverable at scale, infrastructure exists, the recycled material can substitute for virgin without quality loss.

**Why it is circular:** closes the loop on materials.

**Strategic implications:** sourcing changes, supplier relationships, possible vertical integration into recycling.

### Pattern 4 — Sharing
Higher utilization of assets through shared access.

Examples:
- Bike-sharing, car-sharing
- B2B equipment sharing platforms
- Office space sharing

**Fits when:** assets are underutilized in private ownership, coordination cost is reasonable, customers accept access over ownership.

**Why it is circular:** more value per asset reduces total assets needed.

**Strategic implications:** platform economics, asset productivity, fleet management.

### Pattern 5 — Dematerialization
Deliver the same value with less physical resource.

Examples:
- Digital products replacing physical (e-books, streaming media)
- Lighter packaging (concentrated detergents)
- Material-efficient design (less metal per car)

**Fits when:** the underlying value can be delivered without the physical embodiment, or with less of it.

**Why it is circular:** reduces material throughput at the source.

**Strategic implications:** product redesign, often margin improvements, sometimes value-perception challenges.

### Pattern 6 — Regenerative inputs
Replace finite or harmful inputs with renewable or regenerative ones.

Examples:
- Renewable energy in operations
- Bio-based materials (from regenerative agriculture)
- Plant-based alternatives to animal products

**Fits when:** alternatives exist at acceptable cost and quality, or are coming down the cost curve.

**Why it is circular:** the inputs regenerate within human timescales.

**Strategic implications:** sourcing transformation, supplier relationships, customer education.

## Choosing the right pattern(s)

Three filtering questions:

1. **What is the company's primary impact stage?** From the LCA — is it raw materials, manufacturing, distribution, use, or end-of-life? The pattern should address the dominant stage.
2. **What does the customer value?** Patterns that conflict with what customers actually pay for fail.
3. **What capability would we have to build?** Each pattern requires distinctive capabilities. Match to existing or buildable capabilities.

Most successful circular transitions combine 2–3 patterns rather than betting on one.

## Common failure modes

- **PaaS pilot purgatory.** Launching a service offering as a side product without commitment to migrate the business. The PaaS offering stays small; the cannibalization concern stays loud; the transition does not happen.
- **Recycling theater.** Claiming "circular" because some packaging is recyclable. Marginal impact, real greenwashing risk.
- **Refurbishment without market.** Building the reverse logistics before validating customer willingness to pay for refurbished.
- **Dematerialization without value translation.** Reducing material but losing perceived value (e.g., over-light packaging that customers reject).
- **Regenerative inputs at unsustainable cost.** Adopting alternatives whose cost premium customers will not pay, then retreating.

## How to use this in the workflow

In step 6 of the SKILL.md workflow:

1. Identify which patterns plausibly fit the user's business
2. Stress-test the fit against the three filtering questions
3. Surface the capability and customer-perception implications
4. Propose 1–2 patterns to invest in deeply rather than dabbling in five

A circular business model strategy that names 1–2 patterns with credible commitment is worth more than one that names eight without resourcing any.
