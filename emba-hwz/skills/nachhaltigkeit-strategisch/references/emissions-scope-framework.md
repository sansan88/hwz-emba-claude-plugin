# Emissions Measurement — Scope 1, 2, 3 Framework

The GHG Protocol three-scope framework is the foundation of corporate carbon accounting. Required by CSRD, IFRS S2, and the SBTi methodology. This reference is for the executive who needs to understand it well enough to make strategic decisions, not to compile the inventory.

## The three scopes

### Scope 1 — Direct emissions
Emissions from sources the company owns or controls. Examples:
- Fuel combustion in company-owned vehicles, boilers, furnaces
- Process emissions (cement production CO₂, refrigerant leaks)
- Fugitive emissions from company facilities

Generally the easiest to measure. Often the smallest scope for service businesses, the largest for heavy industry.

### Scope 2 — Indirect emissions from purchased energy
Emissions from generation of electricity, steam, heating, cooling purchased by the company.

- **Location-based** — using grid average emissions for the geography
- **Market-based** — using emissions of the specific energy products purchased (renewables, PPAs)

Both must be reported under CSRD/IFRS S2. Renewable electricity procurement reduces market-based; not location-based.

### Scope 3 — Other indirect emissions in the value chain
15 categories:

**Upstream (8 categories):**
1. Purchased goods and services
2. Capital goods
3. Fuel- and energy-related activities not in scope 1 or 2
4. Upstream transportation and distribution
5. Waste generated in operations
6. Business travel
7. Employee commuting
8. Upstream leased assets

**Downstream (7 categories):**
9. Downstream transportation and distribution
10. Processing of sold products
11. Use of sold products
12. End-of-life treatment of sold products
13. Downstream leased assets
14. Franchises
15. Investments

For most companies, Scope 3 is by far the largest. Category 1 (purchased goods) and Category 11 (use of sold products) typically dominate.

## Practical implications

### For a consumer goods company
Category 1 (ingredients, packaging) and Category 11 (use phase, if the product is heated/cooled/washed) often dominate. Scope 3 typically 80–95% of total.

### For a financial institution
Category 15 (investments) — financed emissions — dwarfs everything else. PCAF (Partnership for Carbon Accounting Financials) is the methodology of record.

### For a software / professional services firm
Scope 3 still dominates but is more about capital goods (data centers), business travel, and supply chain services.

### For heavy industry
Scope 1 may dominate (process emissions, on-site combustion). Scope 3 still significant via raw materials and use phase.

## Why Scope 3 is hard

- **Data dependencies** — relies on suppliers' data, which is often estimated or absent
- **Methodology choices** — spend-based vs. activity-based emissions factors produce different numbers
- **Boundaries** — what to include for "use of sold products" when usage varies
- **Double counting** — across companies' inventories
- **Action levers** — many Scope 3 sources are outside direct corporate control

The strategic question is not whether to measure Scope 3 but how to use the measurement. Three legitimate stances:

1. **Measure and disclose** — required for credible reporting and SBTi.
2. **Set Scope 3 targets** — required by SBTi above a threshold; signals commitment.
3. **Reduce Scope 3** — by engaging suppliers, redesigning products, changing customer segments. The hardest part.

## Strategy-level decisions about scope

When advising on sustainability strategy:

- **Do we have a credible Scope 3 inventory?** If not, that is the gap to close before setting meaningful targets.
- **Are we using spend-based or activity-based factors for Scope 3?** Activity-based is more accurate, more decision-useful, harder.
- **Do our targets cover Scope 3?** SBTi requires Scope 3 targets if Scope 3 is >40% of total emissions.
- **Are we engaging suppliers on emissions data and reduction?** Many large companies now require it.
- **Are we redesigning products to reduce use-phase emissions?** Often the highest-leverage move.

## Use-phase emissions — the biggest lever for many

For products that consume energy in use (cars, appliances, software, electronics), use-phase emissions often dominate. The strategic implications:

- Designing for efficiency in the use phase has more impact than perfecting manufacturing
- Software efficiency (cloud carbon, AI model efficiency) is becoming a strategic consideration
- Selling more efficient products may grow revenue while reducing emissions

## Avoided emissions

A separate concept: emissions that the company's product *helps customers avoid* by displacing higher-emission alternatives. (E.g., a heat pump avoiding gas boiler emissions.)

Avoided emissions are not part of Scope 1/2/3 (which are inventory-based). They are presented separately if claimed, with the methodological transparency that scrutiny demands.

Beware of greenwashing risk: avoided emissions claims are often the most easily challenged.

## Carbon credits / offsets

A late-stage tool, not a substitute for reductions.

- SBTi and most credible standards require *reduction* of own emissions; offsets play a limited role for residual emissions only.
- Voluntary carbon market credits vary enormously in quality. Many are non-additional, not permanent, or double-counted.
- "Net zero" claims should distinguish between reductions and offsets transparently.

## How to use this in the workflow

In step 3 of the SKILL.md workflow, walk the user through their emissions profile. If Scope 3 is unaccounted for, that is the first gap. If Scope 3 dominates and is unaddressed in the strategy, the strategy is not credible. Help the user choose a posture explicitly.
