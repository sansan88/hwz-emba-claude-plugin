# IPAT and Systems Thinking for Sustainability

The IPAT equation is the simplest useful framework for thinking about environmental impact at a system level. Adapted for corporate use, it clarifies which levers matter.

## The original IPAT

Ehrlich and Holdren (1971):

**I = P × A × T**

Where:
- **I** = Environmental impact
- **P** = Population
- **A** = Affluence (consumption per capita)
- **T** = Technology (impact per unit of consumption)

The insight: even if technology improves (T falls), if P and A keep growing, impact can rise. The equation makes the math of "growth + efficiency" visible.

## The corporate variant

For a business, a useful adaptation:

**Business impact = Activity volume × Intensity per unit of activity × Mix of activities**

Where:
- **Activity volume** = how much we produce or sell
- **Intensity** = environmental impact per unit (emissions per unit, water per unit, etc.)
- **Mix** = the composition of what we produce (high-impact products vs. low-impact)

A company has three principal levers:

1. **Volume reduction** — usually conflicts with growth strategy. Rarely the practical lever for a for-profit business, though it can be relevant for niche products that should be discontinued.
2. **Intensity reduction** — invest in process, technology, energy, materials to lower impact per unit. The default lever.
3. **Mix change** — shift the portfolio toward lower-impact products and away from higher-impact ones. Often the most powerful lever; also the hardest because it affects revenue.

A strategy that pulls only on intensity rarely produces deep change. Strategies that pull on mix produce business model transformation.

## Why this matters strategically

Sustainability targets are commonly stated as intensity reductions ("30% lower carbon per unit by 2030"). That is a useful metric, but it can co-exist with rising absolute impact if volume grows faster. The strategic question is whether the company is committing to absolute reduction (which constrains growth) or intensity-only (which permits growth-with-improvement).

Honest sustainability strategy makes this trade-off explicit. CSRD and IFRS S2 disclosure increasingly require both absolute and intensity metrics.

## The growth question

A live debate in sustainability strategy:

- **Decoupling possible** — economic growth can continue while environmental impact falls (intensity falls faster than volume rises).
- **Decoupling insufficient** — for many domains (carbon, biodiversity, materials), absolute reduction is required and intensity cannot fall fast enough.
- **Sufficiency** — some companies, especially in resource-intensive sectors, may need to plan for volume contraction in specific products.

Position the user's company in this debate. It changes the strategy.

## Systems thinking essentials

IPAT is a starting point. A fuller systems view adds:

### Feedback loops
- Reinforcing loops (climate change → permafrost thaw → methane release → more climate change)
- Balancing loops (regulation → cleaner production → political pressure stabilizing)

The strategic question: which loops is the company part of, and where can we shift them?

### Time lags
Environmental systems have long lags between cause and effect. Today's emissions affect climate decades from now. Today's biodiversity loss affects ecosystem services for centuries. Strategy must account for committed but not yet visible impacts.

### Tipping points
Many environmental systems do not respond linearly. They have thresholds beyond which behavior changes abruptly and often irreversibly. Climate tipping points (Greenland ice sheet, permafrost, AMOC, Amazon rainforest) are the most discussed; others exist.

Strategic implication: do not assume linear extrapolation. Risks include nonlinear surprises.

### Planetary boundaries
Rockström et al.'s framework: nine planetary boundaries (climate, biodiversity, land use, freshwater, biogeochemical flows, ocean acidification, ozone, aerosols, novel entities). Multiple are already crossed. A "safe operating space" exists within all nine.

This is the macro context. Most sustainability strategy reasonably narrows to the 2–3 boundaries the company most touches.

## Applied: framing the strategy conversation

When working with a user, run this framing:

1. **What is the company's primary environmental impact, sized roughly?** Carbon, water, materials, biodiversity, social? Pick the top 2–3.
2. **On each, what is the volume-intensity-mix decomposition?** Where can we move? What does the math look like?
3. **What is the absolute trajectory vs. intensity trajectory?** Honest about whether absolute reduction is happening.
4. **What systems-level surprises could affect us?** Tipping points, supply chain shocks, regulatory step-changes.
5. **What is the company's posture toward growth and impact?** Decoupling-optimistic, sufficiency, or somewhere between.

This framing produces a strategy with intellectual honesty. Without it, the strategy is usually a compilation of comforting commitments.

## How to use this in the workflow

In step 1 of the SKILL.md workflow, build the case for action through the IPAT lens. State the math. If the math reveals that the current strategy cannot deliver the committed targets, name it before writing the strategy.
