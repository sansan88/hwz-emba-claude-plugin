# Applied Behavioral Economics for Organizational Evolution

Behavioral economics provides the practical bridge between knowing-what-should-change and getting-people-to-engage-with-the-change. The toolkit is small, well-validated, and surprisingly powerful when applied honestly.

Note: applied honestly. The same tools used manipulatively corrode trust. The intent of organizational evolution is to enable people, not to engineer them.

## Why behavioral economics for change

Classical economics assumes rational actors making fully-informed decisions. Real humans deploy heuristics, are anchored, default to defaults, are loss-averse, care about identity and social belonging. The same predictable patterns that produce decision errors (see `entscheiden-unter-unsicherheit` skill) also offer levers for productive change.

## The core levers

### 1. Defaults
The single most powerful behavioral lever. Whatever the default is, most people accept it. Make the new pattern the default; opt-out is harder than opt-in.

Examples:
- Default meetings to 25 / 50 minutes (instead of 30 / 60)
- Default new joiners to a mentoring relationship (not opt-in)
- Default project plans to include retrospective at start (built in, not added)
- Default agendas to one item per page (forces prioritization)

### 2. Anchoring
The first number or example influences the conversation. In change work:

- Bring concrete examples of the target pattern from peer organizations
- Quantify the value of the change with specific numbers (even rough ones)
- Set early commitments at the level you want — anchoring the rest of the discussion

### 3. Loss framing
People are roughly twice as sensitive to loss as to equivalent gain. For change work:

- Frame the cost of the status quo, not just the upside of change
- Make visible what is currently being lost
- Be honest about what people will lose in the change — the suppression of acknowledgment produces resistance

### 4. Social proof
"Other people like me are doing this." Highly motivating, often more than expert opinion. In change:

- Surface internal early adopters; make their experience visible
- Use peer organizations' examples (carefully — context matters)
- Cohort-based change rather than individual change

### 5. Friction reduction
Behavior change is opposed by friction. Reducing friction is often more effective than increasing motivation.

- Make the new behavior the easy thing
- Remove steps from the new process
- Provide the tools or templates pre-built
- Reduce the cognitive cost of "what do I do now"

### 6. Identity
People act in ways consistent with how they see themselves. Change becomes easier when framed as expression of identity, not contradiction.

- "Companies like us care about X" (we already see ourselves as that)
- "Engineers / craftspeople / professionals do Y" (consistent with professional identity)
- Hostile to identity = sustained resistance
- Consistent with identity = active engagement

### 7. Commitment and consistency
Once people have made a small commitment, they tend to act consistently with it.

- Start with small commitments before asking for big ones
- Public commitment increases follow-through
- Self-generated commitment is stronger than externally imposed

### 8. Immediacy
The brain weights immediate consequences much more heavily than future ones. For change:

- Tie new behaviors to immediate, visible reward (not just long-term)
- Reduce the gap between action and feedback
- Celebrate small wins as they happen

## Patterns to watch for

### The "give-them-information-and-they-will-change" failure
Telling people the new way is better is almost never sufficient. Information changes minds slowly; behavior changes through environment, defaults, friction, and direct experience.

### The "incentive-only" failure
"Just pay them differently." Pay matters but is not enough. Identity, friction, social context all influence behavior beyond pay.

### The "willpower" failure
Expecting individuals to use willpower to change against environmental pull. Predictably exhausting. Design the environment so the new behavior is easy.

### The "tell-them-once" failure
Information delivered once does not stick. Repetition through multiple channels, embedded in routines, modeled by leaders.

## Designing behavioral interventions

A working sequence:

### 1. Specify the behavior
Concrete, observable, who, what, when. Not "be more collaborative" — "engineers post a 2-line plan in #plans before starting a feature."

### 2. Diagnose the current behavioral system
Why is the current behavior happening? Look at:
- The defaults
- The friction
- The incentives (formal and informal)
- The social signals
- The identity consistency

### 3. Identify the levers
Which of the levers above could be applied? Usually 2–3 are most promising for any given behavior.

### 4. Design the intervention
Specific, small, testable. Often a combination of lever applications.

### 5. Pilot and measure
Run the intervention in a contained context. Measure both intended behavior and unintended consequences. Iterate.

### 6. Scale honestly
If the pilot worked, scale with attention to whether the same conditions hold. If it didn't, learn and try again.

## Examples of behavioral interventions in organizational change

### Decision quality
Pre-mortems as default before any significant capital allocation decision (commitment device + immediacy). Independent written estimates before discussion (anchoring counter). Visible decision log (social accountability).

### Meeting culture
Standing default to no recurring meeting without explicit purpose. Templates that make "decision needed" mandatory. Cost calculator that shows the dollar cost of a meeting visible to all attendees.

### Adoption of new tools
Default integration with existing workflow. Champion in each team trained and visible. Templates and starting points pre-built. Office hours for help.

### Feedback culture
Default sprint retrospectives with structured prompts. Manager 1:1s scheduled by default, with template agendas. Recognition rituals built into team meetings.

## When the toolkit reaches its limits

Behavioral economics interventions work for behaviors that are:
- Individual / small-team scale
- Bounded in time
- Observable

They work less well for:
- Deep cultural patterns (need the unlearning work in `psychologie-veraenderung`)
- Identity-level changes (need different tools)
- Conflicts between deeply held values (need the conversations, not just the design)

Use behavioral economics for what it is good at; don't expect it to do what it cannot.
