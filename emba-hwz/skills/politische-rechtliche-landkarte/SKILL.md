# Politische & (menschen)rechtliche Landkarte — Strategy Layer

This skill operationalizes EMBA Block 1 Tag 2 (Afflerbach, Oser, Nonnen, HWZ). It combines two threads that the module teaches together because they answer the same executive question: *"What political, legal, and systemic forces should shape our corporate strategy in a fragmenting multilateral order?"*

- **Morning thread** — the political and human rights landscape in Europe since 1945, the architecture of multilateralism (Council of Europe, ECHR, OSCE, UN system), and the most recent shifts: climate-as-human-right (KlimaSeniorinnen v. Switzerland, ECHR 2024), the Council of Europe AI Convention, the EU AI Act, anti-discrimination obligations creeping into business.
- **Afternoon thread** — the SDGs as a systemic diagnostic, spillover effects relevant for Switzerland, and Collaboratio Helvetica's adaptation of Ron Heifetz's distinction between technical and adaptive challenges.

The integrating idea: executives need to read the legal-political-systemic environment the way a skilled board member reads a balance sheet. This skill helps them do that.

## When to lean on this skill

Use it when the question touches: human rights due diligence, ESG / SDG strategy, climate liability, AI rights & ethics regulation, political risk in fragmenting alliances, anti-discrimination obligations, sustainability reporting (CSRD / CSDDD), supply chain diligence, or when a complex problem is being misdiagnosed as technical when it is actually adaptive (Heifetz).

Do not use it for: tactical legal compliance ("draft this NDA"), specific tax or labor questions, or pure ESG-reporting mechanics. Those need specialist tools.

## Mental model: three lenses

Hold all three lenses on every strategic question this skill is invoked for.

1. **Legal-architectural lens.** What does the multilateral and national rights architecture demand, permit, or forbid? Where is it actually moving (case law, new conventions, enforcement)?
2. **SDG / systems lens.** Where is the company part of the problem, where part of the solution? What spillovers does its business model create across borders?
3. **Adaptive challenge lens (Heifetz).** Is what we are facing primarily a technical problem (solvable with existing know-how) or an adaptive one (requires changes in values, beliefs, behaviors)? Mistaking adaptive for technical is the single most common executive failure pattern in this space.

## Workflow

### 1. Frame the question — and check the misdiagnosis trap

Many executives bring this skill a question phrased as a compliance problem when it is actually a strategic-adaptive one. Before doing anything else, run the Heifetz check from `references/adaptive-vs-technical.md`:

- Is the solution known? (Technical) Or does it require learning by the people who own the problem? (Adaptive)
- Can it be solved by an expert? (Technical) Or does it require people to change beliefs/behaviors? (Adaptive)

State the diagnosis explicitly to the user. If it is adaptive, the workflow will be slower and require leadership work, not just analysis.

### 2. Build the relevant legal-political landscape

Read `references/europarat-und-eu-rights.md` for the architecture: Council of Europe vs EU, ECHR jurisdiction, recent landmark cases. Then surface the parts of the landscape that are *moving* and relevant to the user's question:

- **Climate as a human right** — the KlimaSeniorinnen v. Switzerland judgment (ECHR, April 2024) found that Switzerland violated Article 8 ECHR by failing to take adequate climate measures. This opens corporate climate strategy to a rights-based liability lens, not just an ESG-reporting lens.
- **AI and fundamental rights** — the Council of Europe AI Framework Convention (opened for signature May 2024) and the EU AI Act (in force 2024, phased application through 2027) create two overlapping regimes the company must navigate.
- **Anti-discrimination in AI / HR** — bias in automated decision systems is now actionable both under existing equality law and under the AI Act's high-risk classification rules.
- **Business and human rights** — the UN Guiding Principles and the EU CSDDD (Corporate Sustainability Due Diligence Directive) extend due diligence obligations across the supply chain.

For each item, capture: what changed, by when it bites for this user's industry/footprint, and which specific corporate practices it touches.

### 3. Run the SDG diagnostic

Use `references/sdgs-und-spillover.md` to:

- Select 3–4 SDGs that are *materially* relevant to the user's business (not aspirational alignment — actual material exposure or influence).
- Map spillover effects. The Swiss SDG profile shows strong performance domestically but significant negative spillovers internationally (e.g., financial-sector spillovers, embedded emissions in imports, tax flows). Help the user see where their business creates spillovers.
- Run the systems-mapping exercise from `templates/sdg-systems-mapping.md` to identify leverage points.

### 4. Locate the Circle of Influence

Stephen Covey's distinction, adapted by Collaboratio Helvetica: separate the user's **Circle of Concern** (everything that worries them) from their **Circle of Influence** (what they can actually move). Use `templates/circle-of-influence-canvas.md`.

The discipline: identify the first three concrete actions the user can take in the next 30/60/90 days inside their Circle of Influence. Without this, the SDG / human rights work stays at the level of values statements.

### 5. Produce the integrated brief

Deliver, in this order:

1. The Heifetz diagnosis (technical / adaptive / mixed)
2. The 3–5 legal-political shifts most relevant to the user's decision
3. The 3–4 material SDGs with their spillover map
4. The Circle of Influence with concrete 30/60/90-day moves
5. The two or three governance / leadership behaviors the user (as an executive) personally needs to adopt for this to work — because in adaptive challenges, the leader is part of the system that has to change

## Output style

Concrete, sourced, and honest. Reference specific cases (KlimaSeniorinnen, Bosphorus, Vinter v. UK where relevant). Cite specific articles where the user needs to know them. Avoid both the activist tone (which loses corporate audiences) and the box-ticking tone (which misses the strategic stakes).

## Pitfalls to avoid

- **Treating an adaptive challenge as technical.** Climate strategy, DEI, AI ethics — these are adaptive. Hiring a consultant and writing a policy does not solve them. The user has to lead a change in beliefs and practices.
- **SDG-washing.** Picking SDGs that sound good but don't reflect material exposure. The test: would an honest sustainability auditor agree this is where the business actually creates impact (or harm)?
- **Ignoring spillovers.** Swiss companies in particular have a clean domestic footprint but heavy international footprint via supply chains and finance. Pretending the system stops at the border is the most common Swiss SDG failure mode.
- **Confusing the Council of Europe with the EU.** They are different institutions, with different jurisdictions, and the cases that bind a Swiss company come from both — but through different routes.
- **Static reading of the landscape.** This space is moving fast. KlimaSeniorinnen was decided in April 2024. AI Convention in May 2024. The framework is alive; treat it as such.

- UN Guiding Principles on Business and Human Rights (Ruggie, 2011).
