# Adaptive vs. Technical Challenges (Heifetz)

This distinction, from Ron Heifetz and Marty Linsky, is the single most useful diagnostic when working at the intersection of strategy, ethics, and systems. Most failures of corporate response to human rights, climate, AI ethics, and SDG questions trace back to misdiagnosing an adaptive challenge as a technical one.

## The distinction

A **technical challenge** is one where:
- The problem is well-defined.
- The solution is known (or knowable by an expert).
- Implementation requires authority, resources, and project management.
- The people who own the problem do not need to change their beliefs or behaviors — they just need the answer.

An **adaptive challenge** is one where:
- The problem itself is contested or only partly understood.
- No known solution exists; learning has to happen.
- The people facing the challenge must change their values, beliefs, habits, loyalties, or ways of working.
- Authority alone cannot solve it. Trying harder with the existing approach makes it worse.

Most real strategic challenges are mixed — adaptive at the core with technical components. The mistake is to address only the technical component and pretend the adaptive part will sort itself out.

## Diagnostic questions

When the user brings a challenge, ask these:

1. **Is the solution known?** If yes, technical. If "we have some ideas but no one is sure," adaptive.
2. **Whose values or behavior have to change?** If no one's, technical. If the people facing the challenge are themselves part of what has to change, adaptive.
3. **Can it be solved by an expert?** If yes, technical. If "we keep hiring experts but nothing changes," adaptive.
4. **What is the resistance pattern?** Technical work meets execution friction. Adaptive work meets emotional resistance, work avoidance, and casting around for someone else to take the heat.
5. **Are people losing something?** Adaptive change always involves loss — of habit, identity, status, certainty. If no one would lose anything, it is probably technical.

## Examples in the EMBA-relevant domains

| Challenge | Technical face | Adaptive core |
|-----------|----------------|---------------|
| AI bias in hiring | Fix the model, audit the data | Decide whose values the system encodes, accept slower hiring, retrain managers |
| Climate transition | Buy carbon credits, switch suppliers | Change the business model, accept margin compression, retire profitable products |
| DEI strategy | Set targets, run training | Surface and shift the cultural patterns producing exclusion |
| SDG alignment | Pick three SDGs, publish report | Restructure the offering or supply chain to actually reduce harm |
| Whistleblower protection | Set up a hotline | Build a culture where bad news travels upward |

The technical actions are not wrong — they are just insufficient. They become harmful when the organization uses them to perform action while avoiding the adaptive work.

## Working with adaptive challenges

Heifetz proposes practical principles that executives can apply:

1. **Get on the balcony.** Step out of the action to see the system. Pattern recognition is the diagnostic edge in adaptive work.
2. **Identify the adaptive challenge.** Name it explicitly to the team — most resistance comes from people not realizing what kind of work they are being asked to do.
3. **Regulate distress.** Adaptive change creates anxiety. Too much shuts people down; too little produces no change. The leader's job is to keep heat in the productive zone.
4. **Maintain disciplined attention.** People will keep pulling toward technical fixes. The leader holds the team in the harder conversation.
5. **Give the work back to the people.** Do not solve it for them. They have to do the learning. The leader's role is to create the conditions for that.
6. **Protect leadership voices from below.** The most uncomfortable insights often come from people without formal authority. Adaptive systems require dissent.

## The leader as part of the system

A subtle and important point: in adaptive challenges, the leader's own assumptions, attachments, and behavior are part of what has to shift. The leader cannot stand outside the change. Acknowledging this publicly is often the unlock — it gives others permission to do their own changing.

## How to use this in the workflow

In step 1 of the SKILL.md workflow, run the diagnostic explicitly. If the challenge is mixed, name both halves. State to the user:

- "The technical components are X, Y. Standard project management works."
- "The adaptive components are A, B. Those require you to lead a change in [beliefs / practices / habits / structures], and you are part of the system that has to change."

This framing changes everything that follows. Without it, the rest of the analysis lands in soil that cannot grow it.
