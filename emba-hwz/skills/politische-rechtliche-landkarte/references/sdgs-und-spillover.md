# SDGs, Spillovers, and Swiss Corporate Reality

This reference operationalizes the SDG framework for executive use, with the Swiss-specific spillover lens that Collaboratio Helvetica emphasizes. The discipline: stop using SDGs as marketing labels, start using them as a systems-diagnostic tool.

## The 17 SDGs (compact mnemonic)

People: SDG 1 (poverty), 2 (hunger), 3 (health), 4 (education), 5 (gender equality), 10 (inequality)
Planet: SDG 6 (water), 13 (climate), 14 (life below water), 15 (life on land)
Prosperity: SDG 7 (energy), 8 (work), 9 (industry/infra), 11 (cities), 12 (consumption)
Peace & Partnerships: SDG 16 (peace, institutions), 17 (partnerships)

Most corporate "SDG strategies" pick the 3–4 that fit the marketing story. The Collaboratio Helvetica method picks the ones where the company has *material* exposure or influence, regardless of whether the story is flattering.

## Material vs. aspirational SDGs

Two questions force materiality:

1. **Where does the business model create the largest dependencies on social or natural systems?** Energy, water, talent, public infrastructure, political stability — whatever the business actually consumes. Those map to SDGs where the company has *dependency exposure*.
2. **Where does the business model produce the largest impacts (positive or negative) on people and systems?** Emissions, labor practices, financial flows, products' use phase. Those map to SDGs where the company has *impact responsibility*.

Material SDGs are at the intersection. Aspirational SDGs are SDGs the company likes but has neither dependency nor meaningful impact on.

## Spillovers — the Swiss specialty

The annual *Sustainable Development Report* (Sachs et al.) computes a spillover index. Switzerland scores well on domestic SDG performance but poorly on spillovers — actions taken inside the country have negative consequences elsewhere through:

- **Embedded emissions in imports** — the high-quality Swiss life is partly fueled by emissions outsourced to manufacturing economies.
- **Financial-sector spillovers** — Swiss banking flows can finance harmful activities abroad or shield illicit wealth.
- **Trade and labor spillovers** — Swiss demand shapes labor conditions in supply chains.
- **Tax flows** — corporate structures based in Switzerland can erode tax bases in source countries.

For a Swiss-headquartered company, the spillover map is often where the most material — and most uncomfortable — SDG conversations happen.

## Systems thinking for SDG diagnosis

The Collaboratio Helvetica method treats the company-and-its-environment as a system. The key questions:

1. **What is the system maintaining?** What pattern of outcomes does the current setup produce — for whom, at whose cost?
2. **Where are the reinforcing loops?** What dynamics keep the system in its current state even when actors want change?
3. **Where are the balancing loops?** What forces would push the system back toward old patterns if you try to shift it?
4. **Where are the leverage points?** Where can a small, well-placed intervention produce systemic change? (Donella Meadows's hierarchy: paradigms > goals > structure > information flows > parameters.)

A real systems diagnosis takes hours, not minutes. The template `templates/sdg-systems-mapping.md` provides scaffolding.

## Misdiagnosis patterns

Three classic SDG misdiagnoses to watch for:

1. **Mistaking compliance for transformation.** Publishing a report ≠ changing the system. The report becomes the deliverable; the system stays put.
2. **Picking SDGs by photogenicity.** SDG 4 (education) is popular because every company can say something nice. SDGs 10 (inequality) and 12 (responsible consumption) are unpopular because they implicate the business model.
3. **Treating SDGs as discrete.** They interact. Pushing on SDG 8 (decent work) without thinking about SDG 10 (inequality) often makes inequality worse via wage compression at the top of the labor market.

## How to use this in the workflow

In step 3 of the SKILL.md workflow:

1. Identify 3–4 SDGs that are *material* (dependency + impact). Force the user to defend each selection against the misdiagnosis patterns.
2. For each SDG, map the company's spillover footprint. Be specific. "Our supply chain is in Bangladesh; that means SDG 5 and SDG 8 are not optional."
3. Pick one SDG where the company has high impact responsibility but low current engagement. That is usually where the strategic opportunity (and the moral imperative) sits.
4. Run the systems mapping for that SDG. Identify 2–3 leverage points and a 30/60/90-day action set in the Circle of Influence (see `circle-of-influence-canvas.md`).

## Frameworks beyond SDGs (when relevant)

The SDGs are not the only framework. Reference these if the user's context calls for them:

- **GRI (Global Reporting Initiative)** — sustainability reporting, evolving toward CSRD compatibility.
- **TCFD / IFRS S2** — climate-related financial disclosure, now folded into ISSB standards.
- **Science-Based Targets (SBTi)** — for emission targets aligned with 1.5°C.
- **Doughnut Economics (Raworth)** — combines planetary boundaries with social foundation; useful as a single integrating frame.

Pick one primary framework per workstream. Mixing all of them at once produces noise.
