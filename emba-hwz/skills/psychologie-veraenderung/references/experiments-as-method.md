# Experiments as Transformation Method

Berenbold's central proposition: experiments are not just one tool among many — they are *the* central method by which deep organizational patterns get unlearned and new patterns get learned. The mechanism is direct experience, which both unlearning and learning depend on.

## What counts as an experiment

An experiment in this sense is:

- A **deliberate** intervention (not just trying something casually)
- Designed to **test a hypothesis** about a pattern or way of working
- **Time-bounded** (clear start and end)
- **Safe-to-fail** (reversible, contained, not betting the company)
- **Observable** (we can see what happens and what changes)
- **Owned** (named people are responsible for running and learning from it)

A reorganization is not an experiment. A pilot rollout is sometimes an experiment, sometimes not — depends on whether failure is acceptable and learning is the primary goal.

## Why experiments work

Three mechanisms:

### 1. Direct experience produces unlearning
The old pattern is unlearned not by being argued against, but by being out-competed in direct experience by a new pattern. Telling people the new way is better produces compliance at best. Letting them try the new way and notice the difference produces actual unlearning.

### 2. Small scale reduces resistance
A full rollout invites full resistance. A small experiment invites curiosity. The resistance machinery doesn't activate as strongly for "we're trying this for 6 weeks in one team."

### 3. Real data trumps speculation
Most change conversations are speculative. "If we did X, then Y might happen." Experiments produce actual data about what happens. The conversation moves from theory to evidence.

## Categories of useful experiments

### Process experiments
Trying a different way of doing a recurring activity. Examples:
- A meeting series with no presentations, just structured discussion
- Decision-making with a written-document-first ritual instead of slide deck
- Pair programming on a function that has been individual
- Standup-style daily 15-minute coordination for a project team

### Structural experiments
Trying a different organizational arrangement. Examples:
- Cross-functional pod for a specific outcome
- Rotating leadership of a recurring forum
- Removing a layer between specified roles for a fixed period
- Co-locating teams that have been remote (or vice versa)

### Cultural experiments
Trying a different way of being together. Examples:
- "No-meeting Friday" for a quarter
- Public retrospectives where senior leaders attend and listen
- Surfacing failures publicly with no blame, with structured learning
- Mandatory "permission to disagree" turn in important meetings

### Skill experiments
Building new individual or team capabilities. Examples:
- Coaching cohort for a specific leadership behavior
- Peer-learning circles on a new technical skill
- Job-swap weeks across functions

## Anatomy of a good experiment

Six elements:

### 1. Pattern or hypothesis
What pattern are we testing? What do we believe might work? Concrete, not "we should be more agile."

### 2. Specific experiment design
What exactly will we do? Who participates? Over what period? What is the baseline we compare against?

### 3. Hypothesis about what will happen
What outcome do we expect, and what would falsify the hypothesis?

### 4. Observation plan
What will we measure or notice? Quantitative where possible, qualitative where appropriate. Not just outcomes — process observations.

### 5. Reflection ritual
When and how will we make sense of what happened? Who is in the conversation?

### 6. Decision rule
What outcome would lead to: stop, refine and continue, scale, kill?

The discipline of writing each element down before running the experiment is half of the value.

## The 2 a.m. test

A good experiment should pass the "2 a.m. test": if you were woken at 2 a.m. and asked, what would you say is going to happen? If your answer is more interesting than "I think it'll work, otherwise we wouldn't be doing it," the experiment has actual hypotheses and is worth running.

## Common experiment failures

- **Pilot in disguise.** Not an experiment — already a rollout, with no real openness to negative results.
- **Cannot fail.** Designed to succeed. Produces no learning.
- **No reflection.** Run the experiment, jump to scaling without surfacing what was learned.
- **Owner-less.** Multiple people involved, no one accountable.
- **Inconsistent with rest of organization.** The experiment runs against incentives and constraints from the surrounding system. Predictably struggles. Lessons may be more about the system than about the intervention.
- **Single sample, big conclusion.** "We tried one team for 6 weeks; it worked; let's roll out company-wide." The sample is too small for that confidence.

## The portfolio approach

For a transformation, design a portfolio of experiments rather than a single big one:

- **2–3 experiments at any given time** in different parts of the system
- **Mix of types** — some process, some structural, some cultural
- **Different time horizons** — some 4-week, some 12-week
- **Visible to wider organization** — what is being tried, what is being learned

A portfolio produces faster collective learning and avoids betting too much on any single experiment.

## When experiments are wrong

Experiments are not always the right approach. Use other tools when:

- The path is known and the work is straightforward execution
- The risk of failure is unbearable (regulatory, safety-critical, customer-facing in damaging way)
- The change requires absolute consistency across the org (some standards, some compliance)
- The hypothesis cannot be tested at small scale meaningfully

Honest assessment: which parts of the transformation are amenable to experimentation, which require other approaches?
