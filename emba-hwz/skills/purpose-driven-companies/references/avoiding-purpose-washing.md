# Avoiding Purpose-Washing

The single biggest risk in purpose-driven branding is claiming purpose that the operating behavior does not support. The cost when caught is high; the internal cost even when not caught is higher — employees and customers can usually feel the gap before it's documented.

This reference is the discipline that keeps purpose work honest.

## The forms of purpose-washing

### Form 1 — Aspirational language without substance
Beautiful purpose statement; operating decisions made on entirely commercial terms. The gap is invisible from far away, visible up close.

### Form 2 — Selective spotlighting
Genuine purpose-aligned work in some areas; problematic operating behavior in others. Marketing highlights the former; competitors and journalists highlight the latter.

### Form 3 — Cause attachment
Attaching to whatever cause is trending, without organic connection. Pride month for B2B fintech; equity initiatives after major social events. Reads as opportunistic.

### Form 4 — Outdated framing
A purpose that may have been authentic 20 years ago but no longer reflects the company. Founder mythology that current operations have moved past.

### Form 5 — Internal vs. external mismatch
External marketing claims a purpose that internal employees know is fictional. The most corrosive form because employees become hostile witnesses.

## The tests purpose work must pass

### 1. The journalist test
A journalist shadows the company for a month. Talks to employees at multiple levels. Reviews recent decisions. Observes customer interactions. Would they conclude the purpose is real?

### 2. The trade-off test
Can you point to 3 recent decisions where the company chose against short-term profit to honor the purpose? If no, the purpose is decorative.

### 3. The whistleblower test
If an employee disagreed with a decision and went to the press, would they expose a gap between stated purpose and operating behavior?

### 4. The longitudinal test
Has the purpose remained consistent for at least 5 years? Or does it shift with each communications cycle?

### 5. The cost test
What is the company genuinely paying — in margin, growth speed, options foregone — to honor the purpose? If the answer is "nothing," the purpose costs nothing because it has no real claim on behavior.

### 6. The internal coherence test
Do compensation systems, hiring criteria, promotion decisions, capex allocation reflect the purpose? Or do they reflect purely financial logic?

## The escalating legal exposure

Beyond reputation, purpose-washing increasingly carries legal risk:

### EU Green Claims Directive (proposed)
Requires substantiation of environmental claims. Unsubstantiated claims face enforcement.

### UK CMA
Has issued guidance on environmental claims and pursued enforcement against misleading sustainability marketing.

### US FTC Green Guides
Updated 2023; tighter requirements on substantiation.

### EU CSRD and ESRS
Mandatory sustainability disclosures with structured methodology. Inconsistencies between marketing claims and disclosure raise both legal and reputational risk.

### Securities disclosure
For listed companies, material sustainability and purpose claims in marketing may interact with securities disclosure obligations.

The era of unsubstantiated purpose claims is closing. Companies serious about purpose-driven marketing need to be ready for substantiation.

## The internal coherence requirement

External purpose communication must be coherent with internal experience. Specific dimensions:

### Compensation
What does the company actually reward? Pure financial outcomes or balanced outcomes including purpose dimensions?

### Hiring
Are purpose-fit criteria part of hiring? Or is the purpose ignored once candidates are in the funnel?

### Promotion
Who gets ahead? People who deliver on purpose-aligned metrics, or people who deliver purely commercial metrics?

### Capital allocation
Is capex steered by purpose-related criteria, or only by IRR?

### Vendor and supplier selection
Are suppliers chosen with purpose in mind, or only on cost?

### Speak-up
Can employees raise purpose-related concerns? Are concerns acted on or buried?

If the answers to these are "purely commercial," then external purpose claims will eventually be exposed as wallpaper.

## When the gap is real — what to do

Often, the user's company has a stated purpose and a real behavior gap. The honest options:

### Option 1 — Close the gap
Update operating behavior to match the purpose. Slow, expensive, real. The only sustainable path.

### Option 2 — Update the purpose
If the stated purpose no longer reflects the company, retire it. Develop one that genuinely fits, even if less aspirational. Authenticity beats ambition that isn't honored.

### Option 3 — Acknowledge the journey publicly
Some companies have credibly said "we are working toward X; here are the gaps; here is what we are doing about them." Honest framing that maintains credibility, but only if backed by visible progress.

### Option 4 — Quiet the purpose claims
Reduce purpose communication if behavior cannot support it. Communication discipline that protects credibility for future authentic positioning.

The dangerous option: keep amplifying purpose communication while the operational gap remains. Predictable failure pattern.

## How to use this in the workflow

In step 6 of the SKILL.md workflow, run the tests honestly. Surface the gaps. Recommend specific options for each gap — closing, updating, acknowledging, quieting.

This is uncomfortable work. The discomfort is the work; it is what produces real purpose vs. theatrical purpose.
