# Developing Purpose — The Practical Process

This reference focuses on the process of articulating a purpose for a company that doesn't have one yet, or whose existing purpose has become wallpaper. It complements `regenerative-organisation/references/inside-out-purpose.md` with the brand-marketing perspective.

## Where to start

Wrong starting point: a marketing workshop with a creative brief.

Right starting points:

### 1. The founder story (if relevant)
Why was this company started? What was the founding insight? What did the founders believe was missing in the world that this company would fill?

The founder story is sometimes still alive in the operating reality, sometimes calcified, sometimes betrayed. All three patterns are diagnostic.

### 2. The customer "what would be missed"
If your company disappeared tomorrow, what would specifically be missed by your best customers? Not "they'd have to find another supplier." What would they actually lose that is not easily replaced?

### 3. The employee pride moment
What work does the company do that employees are most proud of? When pressed for the specifics, what comes up? Pride is usually pointing at the implicit purpose.

### 4. The crisis behavior
How has the company behaved in past difficulty (market downturn, regulatory pressure, leadership change, public controversy)? Behavior under stress reveals what is actually held important.

## The interview protocol

A working approach to surface inside-out purpose:

### Phase 1 — Internal interviews (15–25 people)
- Long-tenured employees across levels
- Recent leavers (often more honest than current)
- Former leaders (founders, retirees)
- Senior leaders currently in role

Questions:
- When have you been most proud of this company?
- What does this company do that others cannot, or will not, do?
- When have you felt the company betrayed its values?
- What would be lost if this company disappeared?

### Phase 2 — Customer interviews (10–15)
- Long-time best customers
- Recent churn
- Customers who chose competitors

Questions:
- Why did you choose us? Specifically.
- What would you lose if we disappeared?
- Where does our value go beyond the product features?

### Phase 3 — Pattern recognition
Across the interviews, identify patterns:
- What contribution comes up repeatedly?
- What distinctive behavior is named?
- What stories carry weight in both internal and customer accounts?

### Phase 4 — Articulation
Draft the purpose statement. Test it against the patterns. Iterate.

## Draft and stress-test

A useful drafting prompt: "We exist to [contribution] for [beneficiary], by [distinctive how]."

Then stress-test:

- **Specificity:** Could a competitor use the same words? If yes, sharpen.
- **Aspiration:** Is it aspirational enough to motivate, grounded enough to be true?
- **Operational:** Could it guide a real decision the company faces this week?
- **Sustained:** Will it still be relevant in 10 years?
- **Recognized:** Do employees and customers recognize the company in it?

## Common drafting failures

### The category cliché
"To delight customers through innovative solutions." Every company in every category says this. Useless.

### The aspiration without substance
"To transform the future of [industry] for the better." Sounds important, says nothing.

### The slogan instead of the purpose
A catchy phrase that fits on a wall but doesn't guide any decision.

### The committee compromise
Each senior team member protected their pet phrase. The result reads like a Frankenstein.

### The borrowed purpose
Copied from a more successful peer company. Reads as imitation, lands as inauthentic.

## The board / leadership commitment

The purpose, once drafted, must be ratified at the senior level with explicit commitments:

- Trade-offs the company will accept to honor it
- KPIs that reflect it, not just financial outcomes
- Hiring criteria that include it
- Decision rights that allow it to actually constrain choices

If the senior team is not willing to commit to these, the purpose work was theater. Stop and reconsider.

## Cadence

A serious purpose process typically takes 3–6 months:

- **Weeks 1–4:** Interviews and pattern recognition
- **Weeks 5–8:** First draft and internal pressure-test
- **Weeks 9–12:** Refinement, broader engagement
- **Months 4–6:** Operationalization — connecting to brand, KPIs, programs

Faster work is usually shallower work. The corporate temptation is to compress this; the temptation should be resisted.

## How to use this in the workflow

In step 2 of the SKILL.md workflow, if the user is developing or refreshing purpose, walk through this process. Resist the urge to jump to drafting. The interviews and pattern recognition produce the material; the drafting is the easy part.

If the user has a stated purpose that is generic or unmoored, run the diagnostic. Either the purpose can be sharpened from the existing language with the interview material, or it needs to be replaced with one that fits the company.
