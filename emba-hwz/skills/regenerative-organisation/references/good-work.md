# Defining Good Work

If the regenerative organization is built around the question "what is good?", then "what is good work?" is the operating-level form. It deserves explicit treatment because it is the single dimension most directly experienced by employees, and the one most often left implicit.

## What good work is not

- Just compensation
- Just engagement scores
- Just productivity / output
- Just self-actualization

Each captures something. None captures the whole.

## What good work is

A working definition, drawn from organizational research and the regenerative tradition:

**Good work is work that builds the worker's capability, dignity, and contribution — over a career, not just a quarter — in a context that justifies the trust the worker places in the company.**

Unpacking:

### Capability
The work develops skills, judgment, and craft. A career of good work leaves the worker more capable than when they started. The opposite — work that depletes, deskills, or stagnates — is corrosive even if compensated.

### Dignity
The work is performed under conditions that respect the worker as a full person — not just a role. Reasonable hours. Predictable schedules where possible. Voice in decisions that affect their work. The ability to do the work without humiliating compromises.

### Contribution
The work matters — to customers, to colleagues, to society. The worker can see their contribution and recognize it as something they would defend. Work that produces value the worker would not defend (deceptive marketing, predatory product design, externalized harm) is corrosive regardless of pay.

### Justified trust
The worker places trust in the company — for fair treatment, honest dealing, support in difficulty. The company has to be the kind of company that justifies the trust. The Credit Suisse, Wirecard, Boeing cases are partly stories of trust placed and betrayed at the worker level too.

## The operating implications

For an executive serious about good work:

### Capability investment
- Real training budgets, real apprenticeship, real time for craft development
- Honest career paths (with finite ceilings honestly described — not promised endless ascent)
- Internal mobility that doesn't punish the manager who lets a good worker go

### Dignity in conditions
- Schedules and workloads that allow life outside work
- Decision rights at the level of knowledge — frontline workers decide frontline things
- Speak-up channels that actually work (the test: people use them; bad news travels up)
- Compensation enough that the worker is not exploited; fairness across levels examined explicitly

### Contribution clarity
- Workers know what their work is for, who it serves, what difference it makes
- The connection from individual work to company contribution is visible, not abstract
- Work that would not serve the purpose is not done, even if profitable

### Trust earned
- Honest treatment in good times and bad
- Layoffs handled with humanity when they happen; the company's behavior in downturns is the credibility test
- Promises kept; when not kept, acknowledged

## Tensions to acknowledge

Good work runs into real tensions:

- **Efficiency vs. dignity** — some operational efficiency comes at the cost of dignity (zero-hours contracts, gig structures, intensified monitoring). Choices have to be made.
- **Optionality vs. predictability** — companies value workforce flexibility; workers value predictability. Tension is real.
- **Stretch vs. burnout** — challenging work builds capability; over-stretch destroys it. Lines must be honored.
- **Individual vs. team contribution** — measuring and rewarding individual contribution often undermines team-level value creation.
- **Capability investment cost vs. retention risk** — train workers and they may leave. Don't train them and they will stay, less capable.

Each tension is resolvable case by case, not in the abstract. Naming them honestly is the first step.

Then for the largest gap, ask: what specifically is structurally producing this? The answer is usually in compensation design, decision rights, performance management, or culture. Each is changeable.

## Why this matters strategically

Companies that produce good work have:

- Lower turnover, especially of senior talent
- Higher quality output (workers who can do good work do better work)
- Stronger employer brand, easier hiring
- Less catastrophic failure (workers who feel valued speak up; workers who feel exploited keep their heads down or game the system)
- More resilience to economic stress

Good work is not philanthropy. It is the operating substrate of a regenerative business model.

## How to use this in the workflow

In step 4 of the SKILL.md workflow, define what "good work" means specifically in the user's context. The definition is not a slogan — it is a set of operating commitments. Test against the diagnostic; identify the gap; surface what needs to change structurally to close it.
