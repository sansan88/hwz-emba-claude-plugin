# Inside-Out Purpose — Anchor for the Regenerative Organization

Most corporate purpose statements are outside-in: drafted for external audiences, written by marketing, aspirational and clean. They land as noise inside the organization because they describe a company that the people working there do not recognize.

An *inside-out purpose* is different. It is built from the inside — from what the company is already partly doing and could do more of — and projects outward.

## The inside-out test

A purpose statement is inside-out if it passes four tests:

### 1. Recognition test
Can frontline employees recognize the company in the purpose statement? Not aspirationally — actually? Or does it describe a different company that someone wishes this one were?

### 2. Trade-off test
Does the purpose imply trade-offs the company is willing to make? Specifically: would honoring the purpose ever cost profit, growth speed, or executive convenience? If no, the purpose is decoration.

### 3. Awkward decision test
Given an awkward, ambiguous, real decision, does the purpose statement provide guidance? Or is it so general that any decision can be defended as consistent with it?

### 4. Disappearance test
If the company disappeared tomorrow, what specifically would the world lose? Can the answer be named in a sentence that is not also true of competitors?

Most corporate purpose statements fail two or three of these tests. The work is to develop one that passes all four.

## Building an inside-out purpose

A working sequence:

### Step 1 — Excavate what the company already does that matters

Interviews, not workshops, are the right starting tool. Talk to:
- Long-tenured employees who can name what made them stay
- Customers about why they chose this company over alternatives, and what they would lose if it disappeared
- Frontline operators about what they are proud of in their work
- Old-timers (retired or otherwise) about what the company was when it worked best

The output is a list of patterns — what this company has actually done, repeatedly, that has mattered.

### Step 2 — Identify the contribution

Across the patterns, find the unifying contribution. Not "we make X" — that is descriptive. The contribution is one level up: what does our making X enable for whom, that they could not otherwise have?

For example:
- "We make tractors" → "Our tractors enable family farms to remain economically viable as agriculture industrializes"
- "We provide insurance" → "We let small businesses take entrepreneurial risk by absorbing the catastrophic tail"
- "We sell software" → "We let mid-sized companies operate at large-company efficiency without large-company bureaucracy"

The contribution is what would be missed.

### Step 3 — Name the trade-offs

The discipline that makes purpose real: name what the company is willing to give up to honor the contribution.

Examples:
- "We will not pursue customer segments we cannot serve with the quality this contribution requires"
- "We will refuse projects whose timeline does not allow us to do honest work"
- "We will not optimize cost in ways that undermine the people who deliver this contribution"
- "We will pay enough to keep the talent the work requires, even when cheaper alternatives exist"

A purpose without trade-offs is wallpaper. A purpose with trade-offs is a contract.

### Step 4 — Articulate

Two artifacts:

- **The short version** — one or two sentences that name the contribution and the commitment. Memorable. Useable in a 30-second conversation.
- **The longer narrative** — 1–2 pages that walk through what the contribution means, who we are for, what we will not do, what we hold ourselves to.

The short version is the slogan. The longer narrative is what makes the slogan substantive.

### Step 5 — Stress test

Before launching, test:

- **Strategic test:** Does the purpose constrain or shape three actual strategic decisions in front of us today?
- **Operational test:** Could a frontline operator use this purpose to choose between two ways of doing their work?
- **Culture test:** Does the purpose name behaviors that would be incompatible with it — i.e., does it identify what we *would not* tolerate?
- **Cynic test:** Will the most skeptical employee say "yes, that's actually what we do at our best"?

If any test fails, iterate.

## Why this is hard

A genuinely inside-out purpose is hard because:

- It is more humble than typical purpose statements (we are not "transforming the world" — we are doing this specific thing well)
- It commits to trade-offs (we are not equally good at everything; we choose)
- It is harder to copy or fake (it has to be earned by behavior)
- It exposes the gap between current reality and aspiration

Each of these is what makes it useful. Each is also why most companies don't have one.

## Common failure modes

- **Aspirational generic.** "Empower people through technology." Could be any company.
- **Marketing-driven.** Designed to sell, not to anchor decisions internally.
- **Imposed top-down.** Senior team decides, communicates. Doesn't survive contact with operations.
- **Defended after-the-fact.** Drafted to retroactively justify what the company already does, including the parts it shouldn't.
- **Frozen.** Once articulated, never revisited. Drift over decades makes it unrecognizable.

## How to use this in the workflow

In step 3 of the SKILL.md workflow, help the user build (or rebuild) the inside-out purpose. The deliverable is not a one-pager — it is a set of trade-off commitments and a short narrative. The test is whether the user can name three current decisions where this purpose meaningfully shapes the answer.
