# The Cockpit — Diagnosing the Modern Stakeholder Demand on Organizations

The starting move of regenerative organization work is honest diagnosis. The Cockpit (Weibel/Nonnen) is a framework for seeing the demands stakeholders actually place on companies today, and reading where the company currently stands.

## Why a cockpit

A pilot has a cockpit because the airplane is complex and the world outside is changing. A company exists in the same kind of conditions. The instruments executives have traditionally watched — financial KPIs, market share, headcount — show a fraction of what is happening. The cockpit framework expands the instruments to match the demands.

## The eight instruments

### 1. Financial returns
The classic instrument. Still essential — a company that does not generate sufficient returns cannot endure. But returns are necessary, not sufficient.

### 2. Customer value
Not just satisfaction. The actual lifetime value created for customers — what they buy, what jobs the product does for them, what would they lose if the company disappeared.

### 3. Employee experience and dignity
Not engagement scores alone. The lived experience of work — capability building, recognition, voice, fairness, the ability to leave with one's professional life enhanced rather than depleted.

### 4. Supplier and partner relationships
The mutuality of the supply chain — whether partners flourish or are squeezed, whether the company is a trustworthy buyer/seller, whether dependencies are healthy.

### 5. Environmental impact
The footprint and the contribution. See the `nachhaltigkeit-strategisch` skill for the deep treatment.

### 6. Community and society
The contribution to the communities where the company operates. Tax behavior, local employment, civic engagement, externalities.

### 7. Governance and integrity
The actual integrity of decision-making — whether stated values constrain behavior, whether speak-up works, whether accountability flows in proportion to power.

### 8. Resilience and adaptive capacity
The company's ability to survive shocks and learn from them. Not just risk management — the deeper question of whether the organization can change when the world changes.

Some leaders prefer to compress these into 3–5 instruments; the specific count is less important than the principle: read all of them, not just the financial one.

## Reading the pattern

The diagnostic patterns that emerge are usually one of these:

### Pattern A — Financial dominance
Strong on financial returns, mediocre or worse on everything else. Common in late-stage shareholder-primacy companies. Pattern produces strong short-term performance, then accumulating problems on the other dimensions that eventually become crises.

### Pattern B — Stated values, behavior gap
Strong stated aspirations across instruments, weak behavior on most. The "values poster" pattern. The gap erodes trust internally and externally; risk of becoming the next scandal company.

### Pattern C — Genuine multi-stakeholder
Real performance across instruments, with explicit trade-off conversations. Rare. Often family-owned, employee-owned, or cooperatives. Sometimes large companies under exceptional leadership.

### Pattern D — Distressed
Weak across most instruments. Survival mode. Regenerative thinking still useful as long-term direction, but immediate work is stabilization.

Naming the pattern is itself the unlock for the strategy conversation.

## What the diagnostic produces

The cockpit assessment should produce:

- A clear, evidence-based picture of where the company stands
- The 2–3 instruments where the gap between aspiration and reality is largest
- The 2–3 instruments where direction-of-travel is most concerning
- An honest naming of the operating-logic pattern (A/B/C/D)

This is the foundation for the rest of the regenerative organization work. Without honest diagnosis, the rest becomes purpose theatre.

## How to use this in the workflow

In step 1 of the SKILL.md workflow, run this diagnostic before any forward-looking work. State the gaps clearly. Resist the temptation to soft-peddle them. The Credit Suisse story (and many others) is the story of organizations whose cockpit warnings were ignored because acknowledging them was uncomfortable.
