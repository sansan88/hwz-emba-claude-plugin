# Kundenorientierung — Customer-Oriented Company Design

Staudacher's central thesis (2021): most companies are nominally customer-oriented and structurally product-or-function-oriented. The gap is invisible from inside; visible to customers in the friction they experience.

True Kundenorientierung is not a culture initiative or a strapline. It is an alignment of four operating dimensions:

- **Structure** — who is organized around what
- **Metrics** — what is measured and rewarded
- **Decision rights** — who decides about what
- **Processes** — how work flows across the customer relationship

When all four are aligned around customer outcomes, the company is customer-oriented in fact. When they fragment — which is the common case — the company is customer-oriented in language only.

## The four-dimension diagnostic

### Structure
- Is there end-to-end ownership of customer outcomes? Or do customers traverse multiple owners with handoff frictions?
- Are functions organized around customer segments, or around internal capabilities?
- Does the org chart reveal who is accountable for a specific customer's success?

### Metrics
- What is the executive scorecard? Mostly internal (revenue, margin, productivity) or customer-out (retention, CSAT, customer-perceived value, lifetime value)?
- What are sellers and customer success teams compensated on? Booked revenue, or retained and grown customers?
- What metrics make it into the Monday morning numbers reviewed by leadership?

### Decision rights
- Who decides about pricing? Sales (close the deal) or product (margin) or customer success (retention)?
- Who decides about feature priorities? Product roadmap or customer feedback or competitive response?
- Who decides about churn risk responses? Customer success or finance or escalation?

### Processes
- Does the customer journey have explicit ownership at each stage?
- Are SLAs and handoffs documented and met?
- Are customer-facing processes designed from the customer's vantage point, or from internal operational convenience?

## The maturity ladder

### Level 1 — Product-oriented
The company is organized around products. Customers buy products. Sales is transactional. Success is left to chance after the deal closes.

### Level 2 — Function-oriented with customer rhetoric
Marketing, sales, success, support are separate functions. Customer is mentioned in strategy. Operationally, internal silos dominate.

### Level 3 — Customer-segment-aware
Some segments have dedicated teams. Cross-functional account teams exist for key accounts. Most customers still experience the silos.

### Level 4 — Customer-outcome-oriented
Most teams have outcome metrics tied to customer success. Decision rights align with customer outcomes. End-to-end ownership for most customer relationships.

### Level 5 — Genuinely customer-oriented
Everything — structure, metrics, decision rights, processes — coheres around customer outcomes. Customers experience this as a seamless, well-designed relationship. Competitive advantage is real.

## Why most companies stall at Level 2

Three structural reasons:

### 1. Functional career paths
Marketing leaders are promoted within marketing; sales within sales. Career incentives reinforce function over cross-functional accountability.

### 2. Functional budgets
Each function defends its budget. Cross-functional investment is hard. Operating model lags behind stated strategy.

### 3. Functional reporting lines
The org chart determines the day-to-day. Until reporting lines shift, customer-orientation stays rhetorical.

Breaking through requires senior leadership willing to actually redesign, accept the political cost, and sustain the change long enough for new patterns to embed.

## What changes when Level 3+ is reached

Observable effects:

- Customer satisfaction and retention measurably improve
- Cross-sell and upsell rates increase as ownership becomes coherent
- Onboarding speed improves as handoffs reduce
- Sales cycle compresses (less internal friction, more external focus)
- Talent retention in customer-facing roles improves (less frustration)
- Brand strength compounds via better word-of-mouth

The CFO can usually see the effect within 12–18 months of structural changes.

## Common confused signals

### "We did a customer journey workshop"
A workshop does not produce customer orientation. The decisions and structural changes that follow do.

### "We hired a Chief Customer Officer"
Without authority and resources, a CCO is symbol. With them, a meaningful lever.

### "Our NPS is high"
NPS measures one thing imperfectly. High NPS in a Level 2 organization often reflects product strength, not customer orientation.

### "We have a customer-first value"
Stated values without operational alignment produce predictable hypocrisy.

## The CEO question

For the user: "If a journalist shadowed three of your customers for a month, what would they observe about your customer-orientation? Specifically." If the answer is uncomfortable, the work is structural.

## How to use this in the workflow

In step 2 of the SKILL.md workflow, score the four dimensions honestly. Identify the binding constraint — usually structure or decision rights. Recommend specific changes (not strategic statements). True customer orientation is built, not declared.
