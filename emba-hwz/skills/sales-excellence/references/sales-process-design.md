# Sales Process Design — From Heroic to Systematic

Pufahl's Sales Performance Management thesis, applied: meaningful sales productivity comes from a systematic process, not from heroic individual sellers. A systematic process can be improved over time; heroic individuals cannot be cloned.

## What "systematic" means

A systematic sales process is:

1. **Defined** — explicit stages with named activities, criteria, and outputs
2. **Instrumented** — observable in the CRM with leading indicators, not just outcomes
3. **Trained** — sellers know it, can articulate it, can be coached against it
4. **Improvable** — performance data feeds back into the process

A poster on the wall is not a process. Stages in CRM that no one updates are not a process. The discipline is in the operating rhythm.

## The basic shape

Most B2B sales processes have these stages, with industry-specific variants:

1. **Identify / Prospect** — generating qualified opportunities
2. **Qualify** — confirming fit, budget, authority, need, timing
3. **Discover** — deep understanding of the customer situation
4. **Solution** — articulating how the offering fits
5. **Propose** — formal proposal or quote
6. **Negotiate** — terms, pricing, contract
7. **Close** — signed agreement
8. **Onboard** — customer becomes operational user

For complex B2B, the process is multi-stakeholder, multi-step, often 6–18 months. Mapping it precisely is itself the first contribution.

## What good stage definition looks like

For each stage, document:

- **Entry criteria** — what defines an opportunity entering this stage?
- **Activities** — what the seller does in this stage
- **Customer engagement** — what the customer is asked to do
- **Output / artifact** — what gets produced (qualification doc, technical proposal, etc.)
- **Exit criteria** — what defines moving to the next stage (not just "feels right")
- **Typical duration** — baseline expectation
- **Inspection** — what the sales manager reviews at this stage

Unspecific stages produce unspecific forecasts and unspecific coaching.

## The MEDDIC / MEDDPICC discipline (and variants)

A widely-used qualification framework. Useful as a discipline more than as a method:

- **M**etrics — quantified business outcome the customer is trying to achieve
- **E**conomic buyer — who has the authority and budget to decide
- **D**ecision criteria — what they will evaluate against
- **D**ecision process — how they will decide
- **P**aper process — legal, procurement, contracting path
- **I**dentify pain — the urgency driving the decision
- **C**hampion — internal advocate
- **C**ompetition — what else they are considering

A deal cannot reasonably progress past Discover without these documented. Forcing them into the CRM is the systematization.

## Sales cadence

Beyond the process for a deal, the operational cadence:

### Daily
- Updated CRM hygiene
- Time on actual selling activities (vs. internal meetings)
- Pipeline status

### Weekly
- One-on-one between manager and seller, structured around pipeline
- Forecasting commit / upside / pipeline / closed

### Monthly
- Pipeline review across the team
- Win/loss analysis for closed deals
- Coaching plan refresh

### Quarterly
- Business review with senior leadership
- Pipeline health analysis (coverage ratio, velocity, conversion)
- Process refinement

The cadence is what makes the process a living system rather than a documented one.

## Coaching and the manager role

Sales managers are usually the binding constraint on sales productivity. Specifically:

- They were promoted because they were good sellers, not good coaches
- They lack the structured method to coach the process
- They are pulled into reactive deal work, not proactive seller development

A working sales-management model includes:

- 50% time on coaching, 30% on deal involvement, 20% on team / org
- Structured weekly 1:1s with seller pipeline review
- Joint customer meetings as coaching opportunity, not just deal closure
- Win-loss reviews as learning, not blame
- Quarterly seller development plans

Investment in sales management capability typically produces higher returns than additional headcount.

## Performance metrics

The metrics that matter, in roughly the order they matter:

1. **Win rate** — of qualified opportunities, what percent close?
2. **Deal velocity** — average time from qualified opportunity to close
3. **Average deal size** — and its variation
4. **Pipeline coverage** — pipeline / target ratio (typically 3–4x for healthy B2B)
5. **Conversion rates by stage** — where do deals stall or fall out?
6. **Quota attainment** — percentage of sellers hitting their numbers
7. **Customer retention and expansion** — does what they sold stay sold?
8. **Time to productivity** — for new hires, how fast to ramp

Activity metrics (calls, emails, meetings) are diagnostic at best. Don't compensate on them.

## Sales compensation design

A topic in itself, but key principles:

- **Align with strategy** — comp on what the company actually wants
- **Reasonable variability** — typically 30–50% variable for full-cycle sellers; less for technical / support roles
- **Outcome over activity** — compensate for closed deals and retained customers, not for pipeline volume
- **Long-term components** — multi-quarter accelerators for sustained performance
- **Customer health overlay** — claw-back or hold-back for deals that fail to deliver customer value
- **Simplicity** — sellers must be able to calculate their own comp; complex schemes get gamed

Comp that contradicts strategy is the single fastest way to misdirect a sales force.

## The seller experience

Often overlooked. Sellers operate under bad tools, bad data, bad processes, bad coaching, conflicting incentives — predictably mediocre output.

A good seller experience:

- CRM that helps, not just records
- Data that informs, not just exists
- Process clear enough to be improved
- Coaching that develops, not just reviews
- Compensation that rewards what the company says it wants
- Marketing that produces qualified leads, not noise
- Customer success that retains and expands, not just renews

Sellers thrive in a system designed to help them. They burn out in systems designed only to extract from them.

## How to use this in the workflow

In step 4 of the SKILL.md workflow:

1. Map the current sales process explicitly
2. Identify where stages are unclear, criteria are vague, or instrumentation is missing
3. Surface the cadence — is the manager actually coaching weekly?
4. Surface comp alignment — does the comp reward what strategy says matters?
5. Design the systematization roadmap

The work is unglamorous but compounds. 18 months of disciplined process work produces visible productivity gain.
