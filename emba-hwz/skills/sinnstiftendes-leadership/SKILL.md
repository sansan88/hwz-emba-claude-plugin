# Sinnstiftendes Leadership & Dialog — Existential Leadership and Genuine Encounter

This skill operationalizes EMBA Block 5 Tage 2+3 (Christiane Gross & Holger Bauer, HWZ). Two interwoven threads:

- **Sinnstiftendes Leadership** — drawing on the existential tradition of Viktor Frankl, Alfried Längle, and the Vienna school of existential analysis. The thesis: meaning (Sinn) is the deepest motivator of human action. Leadership that creates conditions for meaning produces engagement that no incentive system can match.

- **Authentic communication & dialog** — drawing on Christiane Kolbe's phenomenological conversation method. The thesis: most workplace "communication" is transaction. Real dialogue (Begegnung — encounter) produces understanding, trust, and change that transaction cannot. The skills are trainable.

These threads are inseparable. Meaning is not delivered through monologue; it emerges through encounter. Dialogue is not a technique; it is what happens when a leader genuinely meets the person in front of them.

## When to lean on this skill

Use it when the user is: leading people through change, navigating values conflicts (their own or others'), designing high-stakes conversations (feedback, redundancy, career development, conflict), trying to motivate beyond incentives, finding meaning in difficult work, or wanting to develop their own capacity for authentic dialogue.

Do not use it for: pure transactional decisions (just decide), formal performance management documentation (HR has tools), or therapy substitutes (real distress needs professional support).

## Mental model: motivation, meaning, and encounter

Three integrating ideas:

1. **Sinn (meaning) is the motor.** Frankl's foundational insight: human beings are wired to seek meaning, not just pleasure or power. When meaning is present, work matters and people show up. When meaning is absent, no amount of compensation produces engagement.

2. **Meaning is found, not given.** Leaders cannot "give meaning" the way they give bonuses. Meaning is what the person discovers in their work, their values, their relationships, their contribution. Leaders create conditions where meaning can be found.

3. **Meaning emerges through encounter.** It is in the dialogue between people — genuine, present, open — that what matters becomes visible. Encounter is therefore not soft. It is the operating practice of meaning-creating leadership.

## Workflow

### 1. Distinguish needs from values

Read `references/needs-values-meaning.md`. The existential tradition distinguishes:

- **Needs** — what the person requires (security, recognition, belonging, autonomy, mastery). Needs are universal and largely shared.
- **Values** — what the person *holds important*. Values are personal and emerge from biography, choice, identity.
- **Meaning** — what arises when values are honored in the situation. Meaning is contextual and renewable.

Most motivational thinking conflates these. Untangling them produces clarity. A leader trying to motivate by addressing needs alone misses what would actually move the person.

Use `templates/needs-values-canvas.md` for self-clarification and as a tool for one-on-ones.

### 2. Build the Wertelandkarte

The Wertelandkarte (values map) makes explicit what is implicit. Use `templates/wertelandkarte.md`. For self and (where appropriate) team:

- What do I hold as important, when I look at my life as a whole?
- Which of these values shape my work specifically?
- Which are non-negotiable; which are aspirational?
- Where are my values in tension with each other?
- Where are my values in tension with the organization's actual operating values?

The map serves multiple purposes — self-knowledge, conflict navigation, decision support.

### 3. Engage values conflicts honestly

Read `references/values-conflicts-and-resistance.md`. Most leadership friction is values conflict, surfacing as resistance. The existential approach treats resistance as meaningful information, not as a problem to overcome:

- Resistance is usually saying "this matters to me in a way you have not understood"
- Working with resistance is therefore more about listening and meaning-finding than about persuasion
- When the underlying value is met or acknowledged, resistance often dissolves

For each significant resistance the user is facing:
- What value is being threatened?
- Whose? (Including the leader's own)
- Can the conversation be reframed around honoring that value rather than overcoming the resistance?

### 4. Use the Sinnerfassungsmethode

Read `references/sinnerfassungsmethode.md`. Kolbe's method for surfacing meaning in conversation. Four key questions to walk through with someone (yourself or others) struggling with meaning in their work or a particular situation:

1. **Was kann ich tun?** (What can I do?) — surfaces the field of action
2. **Was möchte ich tun?** (What do I want to do?) — surfaces values and preference
3. **Was darf ich tun?** (What may I do?) — surfaces obligation and constraint
4. **Was soll ich tun?** (What should I do?) — integrates the above into action

The method is not a script. It is a structure for the kind of conversation that produces meaning rather than just decision.

### 5. Distinguish communication from dialogue

Read `references/communication-vs-dialogue.md`. The Kolbe / Längle distinction:

- **Communication** — exchange of information, transactional, can be efficient
- **Dialogue** — encounter between persons, where what is essential becomes visible

Both have their place. The error is to use communication where dialogue is needed (most failed change initiatives, most stuck 1:1s) or dialogue where communication is sufficient (operational coordination).

### 6. Practice the phenomenological attitude

Read `references/phenomenological-conversation.md`. The attitude that makes dialogue possible:

- **Openness** — willingness to be moved by what you encounter
- **Non-knowing** — setting aside preconceptions about the person
- **Presence** — full attention, not preparing the response
- **Letting-be** — allowing what is to be visible, not pushing for a particular outcome
- **Anfragen statt Abfragen** — asking that meets, rather than interrogating

The attitude is the practice. Without it, even good questions become interrogation.

### 7. Conduct the meaning-creating conversation

Use `templates/sinn-canvas.md` and `templates/conversation-design.md`. For each high-stakes conversation the user is preparing:

- What is at stake for the person you are meeting with?
- What values of theirs may be touched?
- What is at stake for you, that you should be aware of so it doesn't run the conversation?
- What questions would open rather than close?
- How will you signal openness and presence?

The conversation cannot be scripted — but it can be prepared for.

## Output style

Substantive. Avoid both the technical-instrumental tone (which kills the encounter) and the therapeutic tone (which is inappropriate in most workplace contexts). Be willing to slow down. Recognize that depth takes time. Don't pretend that genuine dialogue is fast.

## Pitfalls to avoid

- **Meaning as marketing.** Telling people the work is meaningful does not create meaning. People discover meaning; they cannot be told into it.
- **Purpose-washing.** Corporate purpose statements that do not survive contact with operating behavior. Erodes trust. See `regenerative-organisation/references/inside-out-purpose.md`.
- **Therapy in the workplace.** The skills here are leadership skills, not therapy. They support reflection and dialogue, not deep psychological work. Refer when appropriate.
- **Dialogue as technique.** Reducing the phenomenological attitude to a set of moves. The attitude must be genuine, or others detect the performance and disengage.
- **Bypassing the leader's own work.** A leader who cannot encounter their own values cannot facilitate encounter for others. The work is on yourself first.
- **Conflating meaning with happiness.** Meaning sometimes involves accepting hard things. A meaningful job can be difficult. Conflating them produces unrealistic expectations.
