# Needs, Values, and Meaning — The Distinctions That Matter

The existential tradition (Längle building on Frankl) distinguishes three concepts that most management thinking conflates. The distinction matters operationally: a leader who confuses needs, values, and meaning will reliably motivate at the wrong layer.

## The three concepts

### Needs
What a person *requires* to function as a person. Largely shared across humans. Includes:

- Physical: food, sleep, safety
- Psychological: belonging, recognition, autonomy, competence
- Existential: meaning, purpose, dignity

Self-Determination Theory (Deci & Ryan) identifies three core psychological needs: autonomy, competence, relatedness. Maslow proposed a hierarchy. The specifics vary across frameworks but the substrate is universal — humans need certain things to flourish.

### Values
What a person *holds important*. Personal, contextual, emerging from biography, culture, choice. Examples:

- Honesty, loyalty, creativity, freedom, security, family, contribution, craftsmanship, learning, justice, faith, beauty, adventure...

Values differ between people. Two leaders with similar needs may have very different values. The same person may have different value priorities at different life stages.

Values can be ranked, surfaced through reflection, made explicit. The Wertelandkarte (values map) is a tool for doing so.

### Meaning
What arises when values are honored in the present situation. Meaning is:

- **Contextual** — emerges from the specific situation, not in abstract
- **Renewable** — meaning found yesterday can be found again tomorrow, in a new form
- **Discovered** — found rather than created or given
- **Felt** — there is a recognizable experience of "this matters"

Meaning is not the same as happiness. A meaningful job can be hard. A meaningful relationship can include grief. Meaning is the quality of engagement with what matters.

## Why the distinction matters

A leader trying to motivate a team member operates differently depending on which layer they target:

### If targeting needs
- Pay better, give more security, more recognition
- Provide autonomy, growth opportunities, connection
- Address what is missing in the universal substrate

This works to some degree, especially when needs are unmet. But it has ceilings.

### If targeting values
- Understand what specifically this person holds important
- Connect the work to those specific values
- Honor what they care about in how the work is organized

This produces deeper engagement because it speaks to who the person is, not just to what they need.

### If targeting meaning
- Help the person see how their specific contribution connects to what they value
- Create conditions where meaning can be discovered in the daily work
- Honor the difficulty of work that nevertheless matters

This produces the deepest engagement because meaning is the deepest layer.

The mistake is to use needs-language when values or meaning is the actual question. "Try harder, you'll get a bonus" addresses needs. It does not produce meaning. The person who has lost meaning is not motivated by more pay.

## Surfacing the layers

### For yourself
- **Need:** "What do I require to function well in this work?"
- **Value:** "What do I hold as important in how I do this work?"
- **Meaning:** "In what specifically am I contributing to something that matters?"

### For someone else
- **Need:** "What conditions does this person require?" — observable; can be discussed
- **Value:** "What does this person hold important?" — emerges through dialogue, not from a survey
- **Meaning:** "What gives this person a sense of contribution?" — emerges through deeper conversation

You cannot diagnose values or meaning from a distance. The phenomenological conversation is the method.

## The Sinn-Empfindung — the feeling of meaning

Längle and colleagues describe the felt experience of meaning:

- A felt rightness about what one is doing
- A sense of contribution, however modest
- Energy that comes from within rather than from external reward
- Endurance through difficulty because the difficulty serves something

When this is absent over time, you get:

- Cynicism (Frankl: "the existential vacuum")
- Withdrawal
- Burnout
- Eventually, ill health and depression

Burnout is often not just about workload. It is about meaningful work being absent or eroded. The classic case: high-achieving knowledge workers in high-status roles, well-compensated, who burn out. Needs are met. Values are not. Meaning is absent.

## How leaders create conditions for meaning

A leader cannot give meaning, but can:

- **Connect the work to a larger contribution** that the team can see and feel
- **Honor the person's specific values** in how work is organized and recognized
- **Surface what matters** through real conversation, not surveys
- **Protect against the value-violations** the system may impose
- **Model meaning-driven leadership** so others have a referent

The conditions are real work. They are also the most important leadership work.

## A common executive pattern

Senior executives often have:

- Most needs comfortably met (compensation, status, autonomy)
- Values that have drifted into the background under pressure of operating demands
- Meaning that has eroded as the work has become more political and less productive

The pattern produces the high-functioning-but-depleted senior leader. The work is not more money or another promotion. The work is reconnecting to values and finding meaning in the actual conditions.

This skill helps that work — for the user, and for those they lead.

## How to use this in the workflow

In step 1 of the SKILL.md workflow, surface for the user (or for someone they are leading) which layer they are working at. Often what is presented as a needs issue ("I need more recognition") is a values issue ("I value being seen for what I actually contribute") which is a meaning issue ("I no longer see how my work matters"). Going to the underlying layer is the unlock.
