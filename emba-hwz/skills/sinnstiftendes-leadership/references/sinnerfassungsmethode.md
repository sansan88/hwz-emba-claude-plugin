# The Sinnerfassungsmethode — Four Questions for Meaning

Kolbe's method, drawing on the Längle / Frankl tradition. A structured way to walk through a situation that surfaces meaning rather than just decision.

The questions look simple. The practice is deeper than the words suggest.

## The four questions

### 1. Was kann ich tun? — What can I do?
The field of possible action. What are the actual options open to me, given the real situation? Not what should I want — what is on the table.

This surfaces:
- The structural constraints (what is genuinely impossible)
- The structural possibilities (what is genuinely available)
- The honest map of agency

Common failure: jumping to "what should I do" before mapping "what can I do." Produces moralistic decision without grounding.

### 2. Was möchte ich tun? — What do I want to do?
What I am drawn to. What pulls me. Values, preferences, affinities.

This surfaces:
- What I care about in this situation
- Where my energy is available
- The pull of meaning before the push of duty

Common failure: skipping this because it feels self-indulgent. Without it, the resulting action is dutiful but lifeless.

### 3. Was darf ich tun? — What may I do?
What is permitted, what is obligated, what is constrained by my responsibilities. Ethics, agreements, commitments.

This surfaces:
- Obligations I have taken on
- Ethical considerations I cannot set aside
- Permissions and constraints from role, agreement, conscience

Common failure: collapsing into duty alone, treating "should" as if it is the whole of the question.

### 4. Was soll ich tun? — What should I do?
The integration. Given what I can do, what I want to do, and what I may do — what is the action that integrates these?

This is not arithmetic averaging. It is finding the action that:
- Is actually possible
- Is in some genuine relation to what I want
- Honors what I may and must do
- Allows me to stand behind the action with my whole self (innere Zustimmung — inner consent)

If inner consent is absent, the action will be carried without commitment. Other people sense it.

## Why the order matters

The four questions are not interchangeable. The order is:

1. Possibility (kann)
2. Preference (möchte)
3. Permission (darf)
4. Integration (soll)

Starting with "should" (the moralistic move) bypasses the field, the self, and the responsibility. The resulting decision feels imposed and produces resistance.

Starting with "can" grounds the conversation. Then preference. Then permission. Then integration.

## How to walk someone through the method

For yourself or for another, in a conversation:

### Setup
Name the situation clearly. "You are facing X. There is a decision or action ahead. Let's walk through this carefully."

### Question 1
"What can you actually do here? What are the real options?"
Listen. Allow the list to surface. Don't push toward any.

### Question 2
"Of these, which do you want to do? Where is your energy? What pulls you?"
Listen. The person may not have asked themselves this. Allow time.

### Question 3
"What is permitted here? What are your obligations, commitments, constraints?"
This often surfaces what has been suppressed. The "should" feeling from outside.

### Question 4
"Given all of this — what should you do? The action you can stand behind?"
The integration. Not given by the leader; arrived at by the person.

## When the answer is hard

Sometimes the integration produces an answer that is painful. The person sees clearly:

- What they want is not what they may
- What they may is not what they want
- The action that integrates costs something significant

This is not failure of the method. It is the honesty the method produces. The leader's job at this point is to be present with the difficulty, not to fix it.

Often, after sitting with the integration, the person can act with inner consent even when the action is hard. That is what makes the action sustainable.

## When inner consent is absent

If, after the four questions, the person cannot find inner consent for the integrated action, something is being missed. Possible:

- Question 1 was incomplete — there are options that have not been surfaced
- Question 2 was suppressed — what the person wants has not been honestly named
- Question 3 has obligations that are not actually theirs (someone else's "shoulds")
- The integration is being attempted against an unaddressed values conflict

Go back. Surface what was missed.

## Working with this in the workplace

Some adaptations for the corporate context:

- The questions can be private (the leader walking themselves through) or open (with another person)
- The pace must be honored — rushing destroys it
- The leader's job in a Sinnerfassungsgespräch is to hold the space, not to drive the answer
- Use it for genuinely consequential decisions; daily transactional moves don't need it

## A worked example sketch

A leader facing whether to take a new role:

1. **Can:** "I can take the role. I can decline. I can negotiate. I can defer."
2. **Want:** "I want the impact and learning. I want time with my family that this role will compress. I want to grow but not at any cost."
3. **May:** "I have made commitments to my team that I cannot cleanly walk away from. I have obligations to my family that this role will strain."
4. **Should:** "Given all this — I should accept, but only with a 6-month transition plan that honors the team commitment, and with renegotiated boundaries that protect family time. If those cannot be agreed, I should decline."

The "should" is integrated. The person can act with inner consent. The action is sustainable because it honors the full situation.

## How to use this in the workflow

In step 4 of the SKILL.md workflow, when the user is facing a consequential decision (their own or facilitating for someone else), walk through the four questions deliberately. The method is more powerful than it looks because it slows down the rush to "what should I do" and grounds the answer in the full situation.
