# Strategie als Kompass — Adaptive Strategy for Dynamic Environments

This skill operationalizes EMBA Block 3 Tage 1+2 (Julia Straub, HWZ, covolution). The thesis: in volatile, complex, uncertain conditions, the *traditional* strategy process — annual offsite, 100-slide deck, top-down rollout, three-year stasis — produces strategies that are out of date before they are printed. The work is to design a strategy process that produces *enough orientation* and *enough flexibility* simultaneously.

Strategy in this view is a compass, not a blueprint. It gives direction without dictating the path. It is owned by the organization, not just the executive team. It is refreshed continuously, not annually. And — critically — it is integrated with culture, not separate from it (Covolution 2020, Spurlin 2021).

## When to lean on this skill

Use it when the user is: designing or redesigning the strategy process, kicking off a new strategy cycle, running a strategy retreat, drafting a strategic narrative, building a Zukunftsbild (future image), defining strategic objectives, picking up a strategy that has stalled in execution, or wrestling with the question of how to make strategy land in daily operations.

Do not use it for: pure execution-level project management, financial planning models (use FP&A tools), or specific market entry due diligence (different toolkit).

## Mental model: strategy as compass, process as practice

Three integrating ideas:

1. **Strategy is direction with flexibility.** It must be specific enough to align decisions, abstract enough to survive surprise. The most useful artifact is a "strategic story" of where the company is going and why — supported by Zukunftsbilder (future images) and a small number of high-quality strategic objectives.

2. **The process matters more than the deck.** A brilliant strategy badly processed produces no value. An adequate strategy well processed compounds. Spend more design time on *how* you do strategy than on *what* you put in the deck.

3. **Strategy and culture co-evolve.** A strategy that requires the org to act differently than its culture allows will fail. Strategy work that ignores culture is unfinished.

## Workflow

### 1. Choose the strategy process design

Read `references/process-design.md`. Old-school annual strategy process vs. modern adaptive process — they look different. For most companies, a *continuous regular process* with the following cadence is appropriate:

- **Quarterly:** signal review, OKR refresh, strategic conversation
- **Annually:** deeper review, Zukunftsbild refresh, objective recommitment
- **Bi-annually or triennially:** larger strategic refresh
- **Always-on:** environment scanning, opportunity surfacing

The process design is itself the deliverable. Help the user design a rhythm they can actually run.

### 2. Diagnose the status quo

Use `templates/status-quo-canvas.md`. Strategy starts from honest understanding of where the company stands. Cover:

- **Internal:** strengths, weaknesses — not just claimed, demonstrated
- **External:** opportunities, risks, drivers — what is moving, what is stable
- **Customer:** what do customers actually pay for, where do they switch
- **Capabilities:** what can we do well today, what is degrading, what is missing
- **Culture & people:** what kind of work do we do well; where does the culture pull us back

Avoid the SWOT trap of vague claims. Each entry must be defensible with evidence.

### 3. Environmental observation as ongoing practice

Read `references/environment-observation.md`. The discipline: build a small, routine practice of environmental scanning that surfaces shifts before they become crises.

Tools:
- **STEEP+L scanning grid** for macro trends
- **Competitor and adjacency watch** for moves in the field
- **Customer signals** through structured listening (NPS open text, support tickets, win/loss interviews)
- **Internal signals** — employees on the front line see the future first

Schedule one explicit environment-observation conversation per quarter, with named owner and short written output.

### 4. Build the Zukunftsbild

Read `references/zukunftsbild.md`. The Zukunftsbild (future image) is the central artifact of this strategy approach. Not a vision statement (too abstract), not a roadmap (too specific) — an evocative, concrete description of *what the company will look and feel like* in 5–10 years if the strategy works.

Use `templates/zukunftsbild-canvas.md`. Cover:

- What we will be known for
- Who we serve and how
- What our work will look like for employees
- What our economic engine will look like
- What we will have stopped doing
- The personality and identity of the company

The Zukunftsbild's job is to anchor strategic decisions, not to predict the future. A good Zukunftsbild makes 80% of strategic trade-offs obvious.

### 5. Translate to strategic objectives

A small number (3–5) of strategic objectives that the Zukunftsbild requires. Each objective:

- **Time-bounded** — usually 12–24 months
- **Outcome-stated** — not activity-stated
- **Measurable** — clear signals of progress
- **Owned** — named senior accountable

Use `templates/strategic-objective-card.md`. Resist the temptation to have 12 objectives. Five is the max for a humane focus; three is often better.

### 6. Design the engagement layer

The strategy is not done until it is owned by the people who will execute it. Read `references/strategy-engagement.md`. Cover:

- Who is involved in strategy creation (not just executive team)
- How the strategy is rolled out (cascading conversations, not deck broadcasts)
- How feedback comes back up
- How the strategy is woven into daily decisions

The single biggest reason strategies fail to land is that they were not designed for the people who have to enact them. Engage early, engage broadly, engage continuously.

### 7. Run the regular process

Strategy is now an ongoing rhythm, not a one-off. Help the user design and commit to:

- The quarterly strategic conversation (90 min, focused agenda)
- The annual deeper review
- The signal-scanning rhythm
- The way strategy gets revisited when something changes

Without the rhythm, the strategy is just a deck. With it, the strategy compounds.

## Output style

Concrete, owned by the user, designed for their actual organization. Avoid generic strategy frameworks pasted in. Strategy work is craft — the output should feel like a coherent narrative for *this* company, not a template.

Make the trade-offs visible. A strategy that says yes to everything is not a strategy. Force the user to articulate what the company is *not* doing.

## Pitfalls to avoid

- **Strategy as PowerPoint theater.** Pretty deck, no behavioral consequence. Diagnose: which 3 daily decisions are made differently after the strategy than before? If none, the strategy did not happen.
- **Mistaking aspiration for direction.** "We want to be the leading X" is wallpaper. A direction names what we will do differently and what we will give up.
- **One-off-event mentality.** Strategy as the annual offsite. Build the regular process.
- **Top-down without engagement.** The executive team alone owns the strategy. Operational levels see it as imposed, ignore it.
- **Strategy without culture.** Asking the organization to do things its culture cannot do.
- **Drowning in analysis.** Months of consulting decks before any decision is made. The Mintzberg insight: strategy emerges as much as it is planned. Move from analysis to choice faster.
- **No mechanism to update.** A strategy that cannot be revisited when the world shifts becomes a liability. Build in the trigger conditions.
