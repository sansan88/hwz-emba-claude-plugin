# Psychologie des Zukunftsdenkens

This is the layer that distinguishes Krafft's Swiss school of futures research from generic strategy work. The thesis: scenarios are not just analytical artifacts — they are images that shape what people allow themselves to do today.

## Core mechanisms (Krafft 2022, ch. 2 & 3)

### Future orientation as agency
People with a vivid, hopeful future orientation invest, learn, take risks, and cooperate. People with a vague or fearful future orientation hoard, defend, and disengage. The same is true for organizations.

### Hope ≠ optimism
- **Optimism** is a belief that things will turn out well.
- **Hope** (Krafft, building on Snyder and others) has three components: a clear goal, a pathway to it, and the agency to walk that path. Hope is therefore *trainable* and *action-linked*; optimism is a trait.
- The practical consequence: cheerleading does not produce hope. Building plausible pathways and visible agency does.

### Hoffnungsbarometer findings, relevant to executives
- Hope is highest where people feel they have meaningful agency and supportive relationships, not where conditions are objectively best.
- Sources of hope cluster around: family/relationships, personal capability, meaningful work, and contributing to something larger. Money ranks lower than executives often assume.
- Hopelessness, not pessimism, is the killer. Pessimists can still act; the hopeless will not.

### Angst as narrowing
Fear (whether about climate, AI, geopolitics, restructuring) physiologically narrows attention and time horizon. A frightened executive team reverts to short-term defense and rejects strategic options that require patience. This is why "tell them the burning platform story" change-management advice often backfires: it generates fear that then resists the very transformation it was supposed to motivate.

## Diagnostic: reading the team's future-frame

When working on a futures question with a real team, listen for these signals:

| Signal | Likely underlying frame |
|--------|--------------------------|
| "We have no choice but to..." | Fear-driven, agency collapsed |
| "It's not going to make a difference anyway" | Hopelessness |
| "We'll figure it out, we always do" | Defensive optimism — analysis avoidance |
| "If we do X, then we can..." | Hope — note the conditional with pathway |
| "What if we...?" | Imaginative agency — protect this |

The diagnostic question to ask the team: *"When you picture our company in 2030, what do you feel?"* Then *"Where does that feeling come from?"*

## Designing the psychological layer of a scenario set

A scenario set should not all activate the same emotional register. Aim for:

- One scenario that **activates concern** without being apocalyptic (mobilizing energy)
- One that **opens imagination** (showing real upside is possible)
- One that **threatens identity** of the current strategy (forcing reflection)
- One that **is genuinely surprising** in either direction (breaking anchoring)

Then for each scenario, explicitly ask:

1. What does this scenario do to the team's sense of agency?
2. Which moves *can* they take in this scenario? (Surface them — agency is built by seeing options.)
3. What story does this scenario tell about who we are as an organization?

## The hope-grounded narrative

After scenarios are built, help the user craft the integrating narrative the team will actually carry around. The template is:

1. **Honest acknowledgment** of the structural challenges across scenarios — no sugar-coating, name what is hard.
2. **The agency frame** — what we can shape, even in the hardest scenarios. (This is the pathway component of hope.)
3. **The identity frame** — who we want to be as an organization while doing this. (This is the meaning component.)
4. **The first concrete move** — a credible, near-term action that demonstrates the narrative is real. (This is the agency component made tangible.)

Without all four, the narrative does not produce hope; it produces either denial or fatalism.

## Common executive pitfalls

- **Confusing hope with optimism.** Telling the team "I'm optimistic" without showing pathways is worse than silence — it signals you have not engaged.
- **Importing fear from the news cycle.** The doom narrative of media is calibrated for attention, not for organizational decision-making. Strip out the catastrophizing rhetoric before working with the underlying analysis.
- **Performing certainty.** Pretending to know the future to project confidence destroys the team's own future-thinking muscles. Better to model "I don't know, here is how I think about it" honesty.

## Application checklist

When you finish a futures-thinking deliverable, check:

- [ ] Did I name what is genuinely hard, without flinching?
- [ ] Did I make the agency visible (specific moves the team can take)?
- [ ] Did I surface what this work means in terms of who the organization is?
- [ ] Did I propose a credible first step that closes the gap between narrative and action?
- [ ] Have I asked the team how they feel about the picture — not just what they think?

If any of these are missing, the futures work will sit on a shelf.
