# Nachhaltigkeit als strategischer Plan — Operating-Level Sustainability Strategy

This skill operationalizes EMBA Block 3 Tag 3 (Julian Fieres, HWZ). The thesis: sustainability has graduated from CSR-side-project to a core strategic premise that sits alongside time, quality, and cost. The work is to make it measurable, steerable, and embedded in the business model — not just reported.

The skill is built for the executive who needs to move from sustainability *discourse* to sustainability *operations*: setting targets that actually constrain decisions, measuring impact that actually informs decisions, redesigning offerings that actually reduce harm, and organizing for impact over time.

## When to lean on this skill

Use it when the user is: writing a sustainability strategy, setting climate targets (SBTi or otherwise), assessing material sustainability topics, building a carbon accounting capability (Scopes 1, 2, 3), running an LCA, redesigning a product or business model for circularity, preparing for CSRD or IFRS S2 disclosure, choosing ESG software, or designing the sustainability operating model.

Do not use it for: pure compliance reporting mechanics (delegate to specialists), specific tax structures for green investment, or generic CSR communications. Those need different tools.

## Mental model: the IPAT equation as integrating frame

The IPAT equation is the most useful mental model for sustainability strategy:

**Impact = Population × Affluence × Technology**

For a company, the variant:

**Business impact = Activity volume × Intensity per unit of activity × Mix of activities**

You cannot push on "Population" as a business. You can push on intensity (technology) and mix (activity composition). Sustainability strategy is therefore mostly about *intensity reduction* and *mix change*. Naming this explicitly clarifies which moves matter.

## Workflow

### 1. Build the case for action

Read `references/ipat-and-systems.md` and `references/tragedy-of-the-commons.md`. Start by clarifying *why* sustainability is strategic for this specific business — not in general. The case typically rests on:

- Regulatory exposure (CSRD/IFRS S2, EU AI Act-adjacent, sector-specific)
- Customer pull (B2B procurement, retail consumer trends)
- Capital cost differentials (sustainability-linked finance, transition risk pricing)
- Talent and license-to-operate
- Long-horizon resilience (resource availability, climate physical risk)

If the case is not specific, the strategy will not be specific either.

### 2. Materiality assessment

Read `references/materiality-and-sdgs.md`. Identify which sustainability topics are *materially* relevant — through the double materiality lens required by CSRD:

- **Impact materiality** — where does the business have significant impacts (positive or negative) on people and environment?
- **Financial materiality** — which sustainability-related risks and opportunities significantly affect the company's prospects?

Tools:
- SDG mapping (see also the `politische-rechtliche-landkarte` skill)
- Stakeholder consultation
- Value chain analysis
- Sector benchmarks (SASB / IFRS S2 sector standards)

Output: a short list of 5–10 material topics, each with rationale.

### 3. Measure — carbon as the anchor

Read `references/emissions-scope-framework.md`. Most sustainability strategy work anchors on emissions because it is the most regulated, most measured, most material topic for most companies.

- **Scope 1** — direct emissions from owned operations
- **Scope 2** — indirect emissions from purchased energy
- **Scope 3** — indirect emissions in the value chain (purchased goods, transport, use phase, end of life, etc.)

For most companies, Scope 3 is by far the largest and the hardest. The strategic question is usually whether and how to take responsibility for Scope 3.

Other measurements that matter, framework-dependent:
- Water (especially in water-stressed geographies)
- Biodiversity and land use (TNFD framework emerging)
- Waste and circularity (resource productivity, % recycled inputs)
- Social: labor practices, supply chain human rights, diversity

### 4. Set targets — Science-Based Targets

Read `references/science-based-targets.md`. The SBTi (Science-Based Targets initiative) is now the de facto standard for credible corporate climate targets:

- Targets aligned with limiting warming to 1.5°C
- Near-term (5–10 year) and net-zero (typically 2050) targets
- Scope 1+2 mandatory, Scope 3 mandatory above a threshold
- Validation by SBTi

Whether the user chooses to formally validate with SBTi or not, the methodology is the working standard for credible target-setting.

For sustainability topics beyond climate, the equivalent rigor:
- Water: alignment with watershed availability (Water Resilience Coalition)
- Nature: TNFD framework emerging
- Circular: % circular materials, product lifecycle metrics

### 5. Life Cycle Assessment

Read `references/life-cycle-assessment.md`. LCA traces the environmental impact of a product or service across its full life cycle: raw materials, manufacturing, distribution, use, end-of-life.

Most useful for:
- Comparing product variants
- Identifying the dominant impact stage (often surprising — sometimes use phase, sometimes raw materials)
- Designing for circularity
- Substantiating sustainability claims (avoiding greenwashing risk)

A full LCA is heavy. A streamlined "screening LCA" is often the right scale for strategic decisions.

### 6. Redesign — business model and product

The deepest sustainability impact comes from changing what the business sells and how it makes it. Read `references/circular-business-models.md`. Patterns:

- **Product-as-a-service** — selling outcomes/usage rather than products (lighting-as-a-service, mobility-as-a-service)
- **Refurbishment / remanufacturing** — extracting value from used products
- **Material recovery and recycling** — recovering material at end of life
- **Sharing economy** — higher utilization of assets
- **Dematerialization** — delivering the same value with less physical resource
- **Renewable / regenerative inputs** — replacing finite inputs

Not every business can adopt every pattern. The strategic work is to find the patterns that fit the company's value proposition and operating model.

### 7. Organize for sustainability

Read `references/sustainability-operating-model.md` and the **6P framework**:

- **Purpose** — why sustainability matters here
- **People** — who is accountable, what capabilities are built
- **Processes** — how sustainability is embedded in decisions (procurement, R&D, capex, M&A)
- **Practices** — daily behaviors and rituals
- **Products** — what we sell and how it changes
- **Performance** — how we measure, report, and link to incentives

Operating model patterns from Bain & McKinsey research:
- **Centralized** — strong central sustainability team driving change
- **Embedded** — sustainability owned by business units, light center
- **Hybrid** — central team for standards and reporting, BUs for execution
- **Integrated leadership** — CEO and CFO co-own; specialized function is small

Match to maturity and culture.

### 8. Build the strategic narrative

Sustainability strategy needs to be tellable in plain language. Use `templates/sustainability-strategy-onepager.md`. Avoid:

- Greenwashing ambiguity ("committed to a better future")
- Pure compliance framing (loses the courage that makes the strategy work)
- Scope-and-target soup without business model context

Aim for: clear, specific targets; honest baseline; concrete moves; explicit trade-offs; the connection to value creation.

## Output style

Specific. Quantified where possible. Honest about uncertainty. Distinguishes what is genuinely solved from what is genuinely hard. Uses the IPAT frame to keep the focus on intensity and mix.

The BCG framing — "From Compliance to Courage in ESG" — is right: the courage version of the strategy is the one that creates value. Help the user toward the courage version, not the compliance version.

## Pitfalls to avoid

- **Greenwashing.** Claims unsupported by measurement. Increasingly legally risky (CSRD anti-greenwashing provisions, FTC/UK CMA enforcement). The test: would a hostile journalist find the gap between claim and reality embarrassing?
- **Scope 3 avoidance.** Most companies' biggest emissions are Scope 3. Ignoring it makes the strategy not credible.
- **Targets without plans.** "Net zero by 2050" without specifics on how. Hollow.
- **Carbon-only narrowing.** Climate matters most but is not everything. Water, nature, social, governance all matter; pick the material ones.
- **Compliance theater.** Reports that no one acts on, dashboards no one looks at, training no one applies.
- **Bolted-on sustainability function.** A small team with no business influence cannot move the company. The function has to be wired into procurement, R&D, capex, and M&A.
- **Ignoring the just-transition dimension.** Sustainability strategy that produces social harm (job loss in transitioning regions, supplier disruption) creates backlash and political risk.
