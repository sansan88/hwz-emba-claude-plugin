# The Tragedy of the Commons — Corporate Implications

Hardin (1968) framed the dynamic that explains much corporate environmental behavior: when a shared resource has no clear owner, individually rational actors collectively destroy it.

## The classical formulation

A common pasture. Each herder benefits from adding cattle (private gain). The overgrazing costs are spread across all herders (shared cost). Each herder, acting rationally on their private incentives, adds cattle. Aggregate result: the pasture is destroyed.

The structure is general: any shared resource without exclusion mechanisms is vulnerable. Atmosphere, oceans, biodiversity, water tables, public attention, antibiotic effectiveness.

## Diekert et al. (2021) — game-theoretic perspective

The dynamics are well-modeled in game theory:

- **Single-shot prisoner's dilemma:** defection is dominant; cooperation is irrational.
- **Repeated play:** cooperation can emerge through tit-for-tat and conditional strategies.
- **Many players:** harder than two-player; defection by some leads to defection by others.
- **Asymmetric stakes:** parties with longer time horizons or larger stakes have stronger reason to cooperate.

For climate and biodiversity, the dynamics are challenging:
- Many players (every nation, every company)
- Global, long-horizon stakes
- Defection cost is diffuse and delayed
- Verification is hard

## Why this matters strategically

The tragedy of the commons explains several patterns in corporate sustainability:

### 1. Why voluntary action alone is insufficient
A company that unilaterally reduces emissions at significant cost while competitors do not bears the cost without commensurate climate benefit. The competitive disadvantage produces predictable retreat. The pattern: announcement of bold targets, quiet downscaling when costs bite.

The implication: companies that genuinely want to act often need (and quietly support) regulation that levels the playing field. The much-disparaged regulatory push is part of the solution to the corporate commons problem.

### 2. Why supply chain pressure works
When a large buyer requires sustainability standards of suppliers, the buyer is creating an exclusion mechanism: meet the standard or lose access to the market. This converts a commons into a structured market. CSDDD and CSRD do this at scale.

### 3. Why industry coalitions matter
Coalitions of companies committing together solve the prisoner's-dilemma collective-action problem — defection is mutually visible and penalized by reputation effects. SBTi, RE100, EV100, the Net Zero Asset Owner Alliance work on this logic.

### 4. Why pricing externalities is the lever
The economist's answer to the commons problem: price the externality. Carbon pricing (EU ETS, carbon taxes, internal carbon prices) is the most developed instance. When the cost is private, the rational decision changes.

## Internal corporate parallel

The tragedy of the commons shows up inside companies, too:

- **Functional silos** consuming shared resources (data engineering capacity, executive attention) until they are degraded
- **Short-term optimization** by each business unit eroding long-term capabilities (talent, brand, technical infrastructure)
- **Distributed decisions** on travel, IT cloud usage, energy use that aggregate to large impact without anyone owning the total

Sustainable corporate operations require internal exclusion mechanisms (budgets, gates, shared metrics) much as external commons require regulatory and market mechanisms.

## Implications for strategy

Useful applied moves:

### 1. Be explicit about the commons structure
When advising on sustainability strategy, name the commons dynamic where it is operating. "We are choosing to act unilaterally even though our competitors are not yet, because [reason]." Or: "We need to actively support regulation that obliges our competitors to act, because we cannot bear unilateral cost indefinitely."

### 2. Build coalitions
For commitments that depend on others moving with you (transition fuels for shipping, decarbonized steel for autos), the strategy is not just internal — it is industry-level. Identify the coalitions and join them.

### 3. Lobby for the regulation you actually want
Many companies' public sustainability positions and lobbying behavior diverge. The InfluenceMap research is uncomfortable reading. Aligning the two is increasingly an expected disclosure and a credibility test.

### 4. Use internal carbon pricing
Internally pricing carbon (and possibly water, biodiversity) converts diffuse externalities into private signals that affect decisions. The shadow price should be high enough to actually affect capex and operating decisions; €30–100/tCO₂e is the realistic range, sometimes higher for transition-sensitive sectors.

### 5. Acknowledge what regulation is for
The frame "regulation is the enemy of business" misunderstands the commons problem. The right frame: regulation solves a collective-action problem that companies acting alone cannot solve. Sophisticated executives engage constructively with regulation rather than reflexively resisting.

## How to use this in the workflow

In step 1 of the SKILL.md workflow, surface the commons dynamic explicitly when relevant. It frames why the user's company needs to act (collective stake), why it cannot act alone (competitive cost), and what kinds of moves work (coalitions, supply chain leverage, regulatory engagement).
