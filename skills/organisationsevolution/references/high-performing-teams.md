# High-Performing Teams — What the Research Shows

Three decades of research converges remarkably consistently on what makes teams high-performing. The Google Aristotle project, Richard Hackman's lifetime work on teams, Amy Edmondson's research on psychological safety, and the agile-organizational-development literature all point at similar factors.

For the user evolving an organization, the focal teams are the lever. If those teams are not capable of being high-performing, the broader evolution will not happen.

## Google Aristotle findings (compressed)

Google's internal study of hundreds of teams to identify the factors that distinguished high-performing from average teams produced five factors, in order of explanatory power:

### 1. Psychological safety (most explanatory)
Can team members take risks without feeling insecure or embarrassed? See `psychologie-veraenderung/references/psychological-safety-and-trust.md`.

### 2. Dependability
Can team members count on each other to do high-quality work on time? The opposite is people quietly carrying or covering for others.

### 3. Structure and clarity
Are goals, roles, and execution plans clear? Team members know what is expected of them, how to achieve those expectations, and how their performance will be evaluated.

### 4. Meaning
Is the work personally important to team members? Personal meaning, not just stated mission.

### 5. Impact
Do team members think their work matters and creates change? Connection to outcomes beyond the team.

Of these, psychological safety was found to underpin the others — without it, dependability, structure, meaning, and impact all degrade.

## Hackman's three conditions (predating Google by decades)

Richard Hackman's "Leading Teams" identified essential structural conditions for team effectiveness, distinct from the cultural / interpersonal factors:

### 1. A real team
Bounded, stable, interdependent. Not just a "team" because it has been called one. Many corporate "teams" fail the structural test — membership is fluid, work is independent, no real interdependence.

### 2. A compelling direction
Clear, challenging, consequential. Not "do good work." A specific outcome the team is on the hook for.

### 3. An enabling structure
Composition (right size, right skills), task design (whole and meaningful work), and norms of conduct (explicit shared expectations).

### 4. A supportive organizational context
Information, resources, rewards, and education systems that support the team rather than working against it.

### 5. Expert coaching available when needed
Not just at the team's formation — across the team's life cycle.

Hackman's diagnostic insight: when teams underperform, look at conditions before looking at people. Most team performance issues are structural, not individual.

## The compound diagnostic

Combining the two lineages, a working diagnostic checklist for any focal team in evolution work:

**Structural conditions (Hackman)**
- [ ] Real team — bounded, stable, interdependent
- [ ] Compelling direction — clear, challenging, consequential
- [ ] Enabling structure — composition, task, norms
- [ ] Supportive context — info, resources, rewards
- [ ] Coaching available

**Performance factors (Google + Edmondson)**
- [ ] Psychological safety
- [ ] Dependability
- [ ] Structure and clarity
- [ ] Meaning
- [ ] Impact

Score each. The lowest score is often the binding constraint.

## What this means for evolution work

A team selected as a focal point for transformation must be (or be capable of becoming) high-performing. If multiple factors are weak, fix those before piling on transformation work. Transformation through dysfunctional teams produces dysfunctional transformation.

Concretely:

- If structural conditions are weak (e.g., not a real team, no clear direction), fix the structure first
- If psychological safety is weak, build it through leader behavior and structured practices over weeks before expecting experimentation
- If meaning and impact are weak, connect the team's work to outcomes before asking it to take on more

## Designing a focal team intentionally

When picking and configuring focal teams for the evolution work:

### Pick the right unit
- Cross-functional where the work is cross-functional
- Stable enough to develop trust and rhythm (avoid constant reconfiguration)
- Small enough for genuine team dynamics (5–8 is the sweet spot; 10+ becomes a working group)

### Set clear direction
- A specific outcome the team owns end-to-end
- A horizon (12–18 months for most evolution work)
- Authority to make the decisions needed to achieve the outcome

### Design the operating system
- Decision rights — what does the team decide, what does it recommend, what is decided elsewhere
- Cadence — meeting rhythm, async patterns, decision points
- Tools — shared visibility, asynchronous communication, documentation

### Build the practices
- Retrospectives baked in, with quality structure
- Working agreements explicitly developed
- Conflict patterns surfaced and addressed

### Provide the support
- Senior sponsor accessible
- Coaching available
- Time and budget appropriate to the ambition

## Common team failures

- **Teams in name only** — the work is actually individual, the "team" is a reporting line.
- **Constant reconfiguration** — never long enough together to develop trust or rhythm.
- **Too big** — more than 10 makes genuine team dynamics impossible.
- **No clear ownership** — committee-style accountability, no individual responsibility.
- **Senior leaders not engaged** — team operates without air cover, gets blocked by the surrounding organization.
- **Tools mismatch** — distributed team without async tooling; co-located team without shared physical space.
