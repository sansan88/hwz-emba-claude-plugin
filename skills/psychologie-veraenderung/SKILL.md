# Psychologie der Veränderung — Transformation as Iterative (Un)Learning

This skill operationalizes EMBA Block 4 Tag 2 (Dr. Sandra Berenbold, HWZ). Its premise: transformations in VUCA conditions cannot be run as projects with start, plan, and end. They are iterative learning processes at both individual and collective levels. And — critically — they require *unlearning* before genuine learning is possible. Deep organizational patterns are unlearned through positive experience and step-by-step probing, not by decree.

Experiments are therefore the central method: small, safe-to-fail probes that let the organization discover new patterns through direct experience rather than through plans imposed from above.

## When to lean on this skill

Use it when the user is: leading a transformation (digital, cultural, organizational), running a change initiative that has stalled, struggling with adoption of new tools or practices, post-merger integration, building agile or new ways of working, addressing a pattern of "this keeps not landing," or wants to upgrade from waterfall change management to adaptive transformation.

Do not use it for: pure operational improvement projects with known solutions (use project management), or tactical workshops without behavioral change ambitions.

## Mental model: waterfall change is wrong for VUCA conditions

The classical change model — Lewin's unfreeze/change/refreeze, Kotter's 8 steps — implicitly assumes:

- Clear current state, clear future state
- Linear path from one to the other
- Predictable resistance, addressable through communication
- Change has an end point

In VUCA conditions, these assumptions fail:

- The future state is partly unknown — it has to be discovered through experimentation
- The path is non-linear — what you try produces new information that changes the plan
- Resistance is often a signal of legitimate concerns, not a problem to be overcome
- "Done" is replaced by "always adapting"

Transformations are therefore iterative learning processes. The work is not to perfect a plan and execute it, but to design a learning system that can keep discovering and adapting.

## Workflow

### 1. Locate the transformation across the four quadrants

Read `references/wilber-4q.md`. Ken Wilber's integral theory provides a useful map: any transformation has dimensions in four quadrants:

- **I** (interior, individual) — beliefs, mindset, motivation of the leader and key individuals
- **We** (interior, collective) — culture, shared meaning, relationships, trust
- **It** (exterior, individual) — individual behaviors, skills, performance
- **Its** (exterior, collective) — systems, structures, processes, technology

Most transformations privilege "Its" — change the org chart, the system, the process. But unless the matching "I", "We", and "It" work is done, the structural changes do not stick.

For the user's transformation, locate the work in all four quadrants and identify which is currently neglected. That is usually where the energy is leaking.

### 2. Diagnose the default mode

Read `references/default-mode-and-unlearning.md`. Every organization has a default mode — the patterns it falls back to under pressure, the practices it returns to when no one is watching, the reflexes that survive every change program.

The default mode is what the change effort is up against. Without naming it, the transformation will be steered by it.

Diagnostic questions:
- What patterns reappear in this organization after every change initiative?
- What gets done quietly even when stated policy forbids it?
- What does no one explicitly defend but everyone keeps doing?
- Where do bright newcomers learn to act differently after 6 months?

Surface the default mode. The transformation has to do unlearning work against it.

### 3. Distinguish learning from unlearning

Read `references/organizational-unlearning.md`. The Tsang & Zahra (2008) insight: organizational learning and organizational unlearning are different processes. Both are required for transformation, and the second is often more difficult.

- **Learning** — acquiring new knowledge, skills, capabilities
- **Unlearning** — letting go of patterns, beliefs, practices that no longer serve

Unlearning happens through:
- Direct experience of the old pattern's failure (not just hearing about it)
- Direct experience of a new pattern working (not just being told about it)
- Repeated reinforcement until the new pattern becomes the default

This is why experiments are so central: they produce the direct experience that unlearning requires.

### 4. Design the experimental approach

Read `references/experiments-as-method.md`. An experiment in this sense is not an A/B test on a feature — it is a deliberate, small, safe-to-fail intervention designed to produce learning about a behavioral or systemic pattern.

Properties of a good experiment:
- **Specific** — addresses a particular pattern or hypothesis
- **Time-bound** — fixed duration (often 2–8 weeks)
- **Reversible** — can be unwound if it does not work
- **Observable** — outcomes are visible to the participants and the wider system
- **Owned** — has named owners committed to running and learning from it

Use the Experimente-Canvas in `templates/experiment-canvas.md` to design each experiment. The Berenbold (2022) methodology is built around it.

### 5. Locate yourself in the Circle of Influence

Read `references/circle-of-influence.md`. The Covey-derived concept applied to transformation leadership: you have a Circle of Concern (everything that worries you about this transformation) and a Circle of Influence (what you can actually move). Effective transformation leaders work the inner circle, not the outer.

For the user, name:
- What is in your Circle of Concern about this transformation?
- What is in your Circle of Influence — what specifically can you move with your role, energy, and time?
- The first three experiments you will run in your Circle of Influence

The discipline of working the Circle of Influence is itself transformative — it shifts the leader from frustration to agency.

### 6. Build the conditions for (un)learning

Read `references/psychological-safety-and-trust.md`. Edmondson's research and the Berenbold module both name the same precondition: organizations only unlearn and learn when people feel safe enough to try, fail, and reveal what they don't know.

Without psychological safety:
- Experiments are run defensively, with outcomes spun to look successful
- People hide the patterns they should be surfacing
- Bad news travels slowly, then suddenly
- The unlearning step cannot happen because admitting "we did it wrong" is too costly

Building psychological safety is not a soft project. It is a structural condition for transformation to be possible.

### 7. Run the learning loop

Use `templates/transformation-learning-loop.md`. The cycle:

1. **Hypothesize** — what pattern do we want to change, what experiment will test the change
2. **Experiment** — run the small, safe intervention
3. **Reflect** — what happened, what did we learn, what surprised us
4. **Iterate** — refine, repeat at greater scale, or pivot

Cadence: roughly monthly cycles for most transformations, with senior leadership review at quarter ends.

## Output style

Honest about how transformation actually works (slow, iterative, messy) rather than how it is sold (clear plan, executed competently). Concrete experiment designs rather than abstract change frameworks. Acknowledge the unlearning challenge — most transformations fail because the unlearning never happens.

## Pitfalls to avoid

- **Treating transformation as project.** Defining done by Gantt chart milestone. Transformations don't end.
- **Plan-perfection bias.** Spending months on the perfect plan before any experimentation. The plan is wrong; the experiments are how you find out.
- **All "Its", no "I/We".** Restructuring without addressing mindset, culture, or skill.
- **Performance theater.** Running experiments that are designed to succeed for reporting purposes, not to produce learning.
- **Ignoring the default mode.** Designing change as if the existing patterns will yield. They won't, unless the unlearning work is done.
- **Psychological safety as poster.** Stated value, lived absence. Predictable failure.
- **Senior team exempt from the change.** Leaders who declare a transformation but exempt themselves from doing the work also do not do the unlearning that produces credibility.
