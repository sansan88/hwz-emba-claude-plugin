# The Phenomenological Attitude in Conversation

Kolbe's articulation of the practical attitude that makes dialogue possible. Phenomenology — the practice of letting what is, show itself, without preconceptions imposed on it. As a conversation practice, it is the difference between meeting someone and processing them.

## The five qualities

### 1. Offenheit — Openness
Willingness to be moved by what you encounter. To find what is unexpected. To not already know what the person is going to say or who they are.

In practice:
- Set aside the conclusions you have already drawn about this person
- Allow them to be more than the role they occupy
- Be available for surprise

The opposite: arriving at the conversation with the person already categorized. Smart, but problematic. Difficult, but defensive. Ambitious, but politically risky. Each label closes the door to the actual person.

### 2. Nicht-Wissen — Non-knowing
Setting aside what you think you know about the situation, the person, the answer. Not as ignorance — as the discipline of provisional knowing.

In practice:
- "I have impressions, but I may be wrong"
- "Let me ask rather than assume"
- "What I think I know is one frame; another may be more accurate"

The opposite: certainty about the situation and the person. Certainty produces statements. Phenomenology produces questions.

### 3. Anwesenheit — Presence
Full attention. Not preparing your response while listening. Not partially elsewhere. Here, now, with this person, with this situation.

In practice:
- Devices away, including out of sight
- Mental clearing before the conversation
- Body settled; breath available
- Eye contact appropriate for the relationship

The opposite: half-presence — listening while planning the response, while watching the clock, while running the next meeting in the background. The other person registers it immediately, even if politely.

### 4. Lassen — Letting-be
Allowing what is to be visible. Not forcing the conversation toward a particular outcome. Not protecting yourself from what might emerge. Not protecting the other from what they might say.

In practice:
- Comfortable with silence
- Willing for the conversation to take an unexpected direction
- Not collapsing tension prematurely with reassurance or solution

The opposite: managing the conversation to a desired outcome. Producing comforts to avoid difficulty. Steering relentlessly.

### 5. Begegnung — Meeting / Encounter
The willingness to actually be in relation with the other person, not just to interact with them. Buber's I-Thou: treating the other as full subject, not as object to be processed.

In practice:
- Recognize this person as a full, irreducible subject
- Engage as one person with another, not as role with role
- Authentic presence, not performed connection

The opposite: I-It relating — the person as means to your end, as case to be handled, as resource to be optimized.

## Anfragen statt Abfragen — Asking that Meets, vs. Interrogating

Kolbe's distinction in questioning:

### Abfragen (interrogating, retrieving)
- "What did you do about X?"
- "Why didn't you do Y?"
- "What's the status?"
- The questioner already has a frame; the question retrieves data to fill it.

### Anfragen (asking, meeting)
- "Tell me what's been happening for you with X."
- "What's been on your mind about Y?"
- "How are you finding this?"
- The questioner is open to what the answer will reveal.

The same question can be asked in either mode. The mode is the attitude, not the words. Skilled practitioners can tell the difference by feel.

## Common failures

### 1. Performing openness
Words of openness, behavior of judgment. Detected quickly. Worse than honest closure.

### 2. Premature warmth
Generic friendliness that does not actually engage. The person feels managed, not met.

### 3. Skipping ahead
"I know what you're going to say, so let me address it now." Bypasses the encounter.

### 4. Therapizing
Treating the workplace conversation as therapy. Inappropriate boundary crossing. Different role.

### 5. Endless openness
Holding open conversation indefinitely when a decision is required. Openness serves the encounter; it does not replace responsibility.

## The discipline

The phenomenological attitude is not "be a nice person." It is a discipline — practiced, refined, returned to after lapses. Like any practice, it improves with attention and degrades with neglect.

Practical practices for developing it:

- **Mindfulness practice** (see `mindful-self-leadership` skill) — builds the presence capacity
- **Reflection after conversations** — what attitude did I bring? Where did I close?
- **Feedback from trusted others** — what do they observe in my conversational presence?
- **Deliberate practice** in low-stakes conversations — practice the attitude where the cost of awkwardness is low
- **Reading the source literature** — Buber, Frankl, Kolbe — to keep refreshing the framing

## When this practice serves

The phenomenological attitude is particularly valuable in:

- High-stakes 1:1s (career, performance, life transition conversations)
- Conflict and repair conversations
- Onboarding and culture conversations
- Times of organizational change where people need to be met
- Conversations with people very different from yourself

It is less essential for routine operational coordination — though even there, leaders who carry the attitude into ordinary moments build durable trust.

## What others experience

When you bring the phenomenological attitude to a conversation, the other person experiences:

- Being seen as a full person, not a function
- Time slowing down — the conversation has room
- Being able to say what is actually moving in them
- Not having to perform or defend
- A subtle but real shift in trust

These effects compound over many conversations into the relationship the user is trying to build.
