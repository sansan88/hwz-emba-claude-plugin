# Values Conflicts and the Phenomenology of Resistance

Most leadership friction is values conflict — but the conflict is often invisible because the participants are not aware of the values at stake. Surfacing them changes the conversation dramatically.

## Types of values conflict

### 1. Internal — values in tension within one person
A leader values both achievement (push for results) and care (don't burn out the team). The values pull in different directions. Most adults carry several such tensions.

### 2. Interpersonal — one person's values vs. another's
A team lead values craftsmanship; a product manager values speed-to-market. Both are legitimate values, in genuine tension in the work.

### 3. Person-organization — one person's values vs. the operating values of the organization
The most consequential. An employee values honest dealing; the organization rewards political maneuvering. The friction is structural.

### 4. Intergroup — different value sets across groups
Engineering values precision; sales values flexibility. Each group's values shape its perspective on the situation.

## Resistance as information

The existential approach to resistance (in change, in conflict, in the difficult conversation): treat it as meaningful, not as obstacle.

Resistance usually says one of:

- "Something I value is being threatened."
- "Something important to me has not been understood."
- "What you are asking does not align with what is true for me."
- "I have experience or knowledge that has not been considered."

When you treat resistance as "obstacle to overcome," you bypass the information. The decision quality suffers. The relationship suffers. The change does not stick.

When you treat resistance as "what is this person trying to protect or honor?", the conversation shifts. You may still decide to proceed in a way the other person doesn't like — but you do so knowing what you are setting aside, and they feel heard.

## The pattern of unaddressed values conflict

Untreated values conflict tends to escalate predictably:

1. **First** — disagreement on substance ("we should do X / no we should do Y")
2. **Second** — disagreement on framing ("the real issue is A / no it's B")
3. **Third** — disagreement on intent ("you don't care about A / you're not serious about B")
4. **Fourth** — character attribution ("you're a careless person / you're a rigid person")
5. **Fifth** — relationship breakdown

The escalation can be reversed at any stage by stepping back to surface the values. The earlier, the easier.

## Working with resistance — practical sequence

### 1. Notice the resistance
Watch for: persistent counter-arguments, energy drop, fading commitment, repeated avoidance, withdrawal. Resistance is often non-verbal before verbal.

### 2. Stop pushing on the substance
The reflex is to argue harder. Counter-productive. Step back.

### 3. Surface the underlying value
Ask, with genuine curiosity:
- "What's important to you that we may not be seeing?"
- "What feels at stake for you here?"
- "What would have to be true for this to be okay with you?"

### 4. Listen, not just for content but for what is held important
The person may not name the value explicitly. You may have to name it tentatively: "It sounds like X matters to you here — is that right?"

### 5. Acknowledge the value as legitimate
You are not yet agreeing on the decision. You are acknowledging that this thing matters and that you have heard it. This step alone often shifts the dynamic.

### 6. Look for the integrating move
Sometimes both values can be honored with a different approach. Sometimes a clear trade-off has to be made, and the trade-off can be made openly rather than smuggled.

### 7. Decide explicitly, with values in view
"We are choosing X. We are doing so accepting that this puts pressure on Y, which we know matters to you. Here is what we are doing to honor Y in this choice."

## When the conflict is structural

Sometimes the values conflict is between the person and the system. The conversation can surface this without solving it:

- "Your values around X are real and valid. The organization is currently optimized around Y. That conflict will not disappear from this conversation."
- "What is in your circle of influence to advocate for X? What can I do as your manager?"
- "What is the threshold beyond which this conflict becomes untenable for you?"

The honest naming of structural conflict is itself respectful. Pretending the conflict isn't there is the disrespect.

## The leader's own values in conflict situations

Often forgotten: the leader has values at stake too. Often they include:

- Wanting to be seen as effective
- Wanting to maintain authority
- Wanting to be liked
- Wanting to avoid difficulty
- Genuine substantive values about the decision at hand

When the leader's own values are unexamined, they distort the conversation. The leader pushes harder on the substance because backing down threatens "being effective" — not because the substance demands it.

The discipline: before going into a values conflict, surface your own. What is at stake for you that you should be aware of?

## Persistence of values

Values change slowly. Trying to "convince" someone to drop a deeply held value usually fails. It also damages the relationship.

The more productive moves:

- Understand what the value protects
- Find ways to honor it in the situation, where possible
- Make trade-offs explicit when honoring is not possible
- Build relationship credit that may, over years, allow some value evolution through experience

Values that change quickly often weren't deeply held.

## How to use this in the workflow

In step 3 of the SKILL.md workflow:

1. Identify the values conflict the user is facing (often hidden underneath the surface dispute)
2. Help the user surface their own values at stake — not just the other person's
3. Design the conversation that addresses the values, not just the substance
4. Plan for an explicit, honest decision that names the trade-offs

A leader who can navigate values conflict authentically becomes someone people trust to bring hard problems to.
