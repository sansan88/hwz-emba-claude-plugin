# Engaging the Organization in Strategy

A strategy that is not owned by the people who execute it produces compliance, not commitment. Engagement is not a communication problem; it is a design problem.

## The participation pyramid

A useful design heuristic:

### Top — strategic synthesis
Owned by the executive team. They are accountable for the final compass, for the trade-offs, for the resourcing decisions. Around 5–10 people.

### Upper-middle — strategic shaping
Senior leaders one level below the executive team. They contribute to status quo analysis, environmental observation, Zukunftsbild iteration, objective definition. Around 20–60 people in a mid-sized organization.

### Middle — strategic refinement
Department heads and senior individual contributors. They test the strategy against operational reality, surface implementation challenges, refine the cascade. Around 100–300 people.

### Wide base — strategic operation
The wider organization. They live the strategy in daily decisions. They surface front-line signals. They need to understand the compass clearly enough to use it.

Each layer has a different role. Engaging only the top produces a strategy out of touch with operations. Engaging only widely produces consensus without direction. Match engagement to layer.

## The rollout sequence

Three common rollout patterns:

### Pattern A — Cascade conversations
The executive team articulates the strategy. Each member then holds structured conversations with their direct reports, who in turn hold conversations with theirs. Each conversation includes both transmission and feedback channels.

**Strengths:** Personal, contextual, allows feedback. Builds ownership at each level.
**Weaknesses:** Slow. Quality varies by manager. Requires real management capability.
**Best when:** The strategy involves significant change; the culture values dialogue.

### Pattern B — Town hall + deep dives
A company-wide event for the headline narrative. Followed by team-level deep-dives where managers explore implications for their work.

**Strengths:** Fast initial reach. Single source of truth for the headline.
**Weaknesses:** Risk of one-way broadcast. Requires strong team-level follow-through.
**Best when:** The strategy is largely a refinement of existing direction; speed matters.

### Pattern C — Co-creation
The strategy is built with much broader participation from the start — diagonal slices of the organization help shape it. The "rollout" is more like an unveiling of work many have already touched.

**Strengths:** Highest ownership. Strategy is shaped by operational reality.
**Weaknesses:** Slow. Requires real executive willingness to be shaped. Risk of compromise toward mediocrity.
**Best when:** Strategy is a significant pivot requiring belief; culture is collaborative; time is available.

## What the cascade conversation looks like

For pattern A, the manager-led conversation has a structure worth borrowing:

1. **Where we are** — the honest status quo
2. **Where we are going** — the Zukunftsbild and key objectives, in plain language
3. **Why this is the right direction** — the reasoning, including what we considered and chose not to do
4. **What this means for our team / function** — the specific implications
5. **What I (the manager) am committing to** — concrete near-term moves
6. **What I need from you** — specific asks
7. **What is hard about this** — honest acknowledgment of difficulty
8. **What you are seeing / worrying about** — invited feedback

Time: 60–90 minutes. Not a deck. A conversation.

## Engagement during, not just after

The deepest engagement happens *during* the strategy creation, not after. Specific practices:

- **Diagonal-slice working groups** on the major strategic questions
- **Front-line voice sessions** during the status-quo phase
- **Customer/employee/partner interviews** as raw material
- **Open consultation periods** on draft Zukunftsbild
- **Pre-rollout pressure tests** with skeptical senior leaders

Each of these expands ownership and improves the strategy in different ways.

## The strategic narrative

The output of all this work is a single coherent narrative that anyone in the company can repeat. Not the slogan, not the slide — the story.

Structure:

1. **The situation we are in** (status quo)
2. **What is moving in the world that matters** (environment)
3. **What we want to be in 5–10 years** (Zukunftsbild headline)
4. **Why this matters** (purpose and identity)
5. **The choices we are making and not making** (strategy)
6. **What this asks of us** (cultural and behavioral implications)
7. **The first concrete moves** (what is changing now)

Senior leaders should be able to tell this story in 7 minutes, walking. If they cannot, the strategy is not yet shared.

## Common engagement failures

- **Engagement as PR.** Communicating the strategy without changing it based on what is heard. Erodes trust.
- **Death by deck.** Distributing decks as engagement. Slides are not conversations.
- **Engagement only at rollout.** Asking for input after the strategy is final. Insulting.
- **Diversity of voice without diversity of power.** Including operational voices but giving them no weight in decisions.
- **One-time event.** Engagement as a launch, not a continuous practice.

## How to use this in the workflow

In step 6 of the SKILL.md workflow, design the engagement architecture *before* writing the deck. Pick the rollout pattern that matches the magnitude of change and the culture. Build the rhythm of ongoing strategic conversation, not just the launch event.
