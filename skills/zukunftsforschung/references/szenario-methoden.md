# Scenario Construction Methods

Two methods cover the vast majority of practical executive-level scenario work. Use the decision rules at the top to pick; do not run both for the same question.

## When to use which

- **2x2 Axes of Uncertainty** — Use when, after the trend analysis, two uncertainties clearly dominate the rest in impact-on-decision. Best for sharp, decision-forcing scenario sets.
- **Dator's Four Generic Futures** — Use when no two uncertainties dominate, or when the question is broad ("future of the industry") rather than tied to a specific decision. Best for opening imagination and breaking single-path anchoring.

If in doubt, default to the 2x2 — its outputs translate more directly into executive moves.

## Method 1: 2x2 Axes of Uncertainty

### Step A — Pick the axes
From the trend map, identify the two uncertainties that are simultaneously:
- High *impact* on the user's strategic decision
- High *uncertainty* in direction or pace

Phrase each as a continuum with two named endpoints. Good axes are crisp opposites. Example pair for a B2B SaaS strategy:
- Axis 1: AI agent adoption in enterprise procurement — **slow & cautious ←→ fast & dominant**
- Axis 2: Regulatory regime for AI data use in EU — **permissive ←→ restrictive**

Bad axes to avoid:
- Anything that collapses to "good vs bad" — produces lazy scenarios
- Two correlated axes (e.g., AI adoption + AI investment) — produces only two real quadrants
- Axes that are not actually uncertain (e.g., "demographics aging" — direction is known)

### Step B — Populate the four quadrants
For each quadrant, write:
1. A name (memorable, neutral, evocative — not heroic or villainous)
2. A one-paragraph narrative of the world in this quadrant by horizon year
3. Three to five distinctive features (markets, customer behavior, competitive landscape, regulation, technology state)
4. The implication for the user's decision in this scenario

### Step C — Stress-test
Before locking, run two tests:
- **Plausibility** — could a thoughtful industry observer accept each scenario as a coherent future, even if they think it unlikely? If not, refine.
- **Differentiation** — does each quadrant actually imply different moves? If two scenarios produce the same strategic answer, collapse them and pick a different axis.

## Method 2: Dator's Four Generic Futures

Hawaii futurist Jim Dator's claim: nearly any forecast can be categorized as one of four archetypes. Using all four prevents the typical narrowing onto a single dominant image.

### The four archetypes

- **Continued Growth** — current trends and assumptions continue, scaled. The "more of the same" future. Often the implicit default in business plans.
- **Collapse** — a structural system fails (financial, ecological, geopolitical, social trust). Not necessarily total — a domain-specific collapse is more common.
- **Discipline** — society or the system imposes constraints to avoid collapse. Regulation, rationing, deliberate de-growth in some dimension.
- **Transformation** — a paradigm shift (technological, social, spiritual) reshapes the rules. AI agents reshape work; energy abundance reshapes geopolitics.

### Procedure
For each archetype, construct the scenario specific to the user's context:
1. What does "continued growth" of the current business look like in 2032? Honestly extrapolate.
2. What is the most plausible *partial collapse* relevant to this business? (Customer segment, supply chain, regulatory regime, talent pool.)
3. What discipline regime — externally imposed or self-imposed — would reshape the operating environment?
4. What transformation, if it happened, would make the current business model obsolete or extraordinarily valuable?

The discipline of using *all four* is the value: executives gravitate toward Continued Growth + an emotionally charged Collapse scenario, and skip Discipline and Transformation, which are usually where the most useful options live.

## Output structure (use for both methods)

For each scenario produce:

```
Name: <short, memorable, neutral>
One-line headline: <what defines this world>
Narrative (1 paragraph): <story of how we got here and what daily reality looks like>
Distinctive features:
  - Market: ...
  - Customers: ...
  - Competition: ...
  - Regulation: ...
  - Technology: ...
Signals this is materializing:
  - <observable, time-stamped indicator>
  - <observable, time-stamped indicator>
Implications for our decision:
  - <crisp, action-oriented>
Hope-and-agency read:
  - What this scenario activates in the team
  - What agency we retain in it
```

## Frequency

3 scenarios feel underspecified; 4 is the sweet spot; 5+ tend to overload the executive audience. If the trend landscape genuinely demands more, group into 4 with explicit named variants inside one or two of them.
